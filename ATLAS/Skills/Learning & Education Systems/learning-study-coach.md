Full system developed by Dr. Justin Sung (iCanStudy). Ash applies + extends it for medical exam prep. This skill gives Claude deep understanding to coach Jani at first-principles level.

## The Architecture: One Core Idea

Everything in iCanStudy flows from one principle:

**Memory and understanding are symptoms of knowledge networks. Networks form through higher-order thinking (comparing, contrasting, evaluating relationships). Not through repetition.**

All techniques exist to force higher-order thinking as early as possible, starting with the logic layer, working outward.

---

## The Bear Hunter System (BHS)

The core study method. Three phases: AIM → SHOOT → SKIN

### AIM (Pre-learning)

Purpose: Create relevance before encountering content. Prime the brain to ask questions, not absorb answers.

How:
1. Collect keywords from the topic (your own, not the textbook's order)
2. Ask for each: **"Why is this important? How does it relate to [other keyword]?"** — never "What is X?"
3. Form a hypothesis chunk structure (which concepts belong together and why?)
4. Skim material just enough to test the hypothesis

First principles behind AIM:
- **Elaborative Interrogation** (Willoughby & Wood, 1994): Why-questions produce significantly better retention than what-questions by forcing semantic encoding
- **Generation Effect** (Slamecka & Graf, 1978): Generating your own structure before reading activates deeper encoding than reading structure provided
- **Priming** (cognitive psychology): Activating relevant prior knowledge before encoding new information increases schema connection density
- **Cognitive Load Theory** (Sweller, 1988): Chunking reduces extraneous load, freeing working memory for germane load (schema formation)

Common mistakes:
- Prechunking: taking the textbook's structure instead of forming your own
- Spiderwebbing: drawing too many connections without evaluating their importance
- What-questions: "What is mitosis?" instead of "Why does mitosis matter for what the exam wants?"

---

### SHOOT (Encoding)

Purpose: Build the knowledge network. Start with Logic Layer, add depth layer by layer.

The 4 Layers of Learning (always in this order):
1. **Logic Layer** — Why is this important? How do the biggest concepts connect? (backbone, 3-7 main chunks)
2. **Concepts Layer** — What is it? How does it happen? (mechanisms, processes)
3. **Important Details** — Details that help you understand the concept above (Layer 3 if it helps you understand Layer 2, otherwise skip)
4. **Arbitrary Details** — Rote facts needed only for the exam. Only ~10-20% of total material. Goes on flashcards.

Key insight: most people start with Layer 4 and try to memorize their way up. iCanStudy starts with Layer 1 and works outward. Higher layers automatically fill in lower layers.

**GRINDE Map** — the output format for a correct mindmap:
- **G**rouped: importance-based chunks, not textbook sections
- **R**eflective: shows the layers of learning, not just information hierarchy
- **I**nterconnected: lateral relationships between chunks, not just top-down
- **N**on-verbal: spatial, visual, drawings — activates more memory nodes
- **D**irectional: clear backbone with flow of ideas (Skin step creates this)
- **E**mphasized: important things look different from details

**VPReFRE** for notes:
Visual / Processed / Relational / Freehand / Reflective / Efficient

First principles behind SHOOT:
- **Levels of Processing Theory** (Craik & Lockhart, 1972): Semantic processing (meaning, relationships) produces stronger memory traces than structural or phonological processing
- **Schema Theory** (Piaget; Bartlett, 1932): New information anchors to existing schemas. More connections = more retrieval pathways
- **Dual Coding Theory** (Paivio, 1971): Verbal + visual encoding creates more memory pathways than verbal alone
- **Chunking** (Miller, 1956): Working memory holds 4±1 chunks. Chunking by importance = bigger meaningful units
- **Desirable Difficulties** (Bjork, 1994): Difficulty during encoding (making your own chunks, not copying textbook structure) improves long-term retention even if it feels harder

---

### SKIN (Refinement/Evaluation)

Purpose: Evaluate and simplify the GRINDE map. This is Bloom's Evaluate level — the highest order.

What SKIN does:
- Prune the map: remove lines that aren't truly important
- Rebuild chunk structure if a simpler, more intuitive way exists
- Ensure backbone is visible and flows logically
- Add directional arrows between chunks

Importance: Most students never reach SKIN. It's what separates good mindmaps from expert mindmaps. The "right" chunk structure usually involves seeing the topic from a different perspective than how it was taught.

Key test: Could someone who has never studied this topic follow the logic of your map?

---

### Hipshot (Advanced)

For when AIM-SHOOT cycling is near unconscious competence. Mental AIM immediately followed by SHOOT — no written hypothesis step. The cycle is internal. Goes faster. Requires months of BHS practice first.

## Importance-Based Chunking

The single most critical skill in the entire system.

Not: "What are the categories?" (textbook chunking / prechunking)
Yes: "Why is this important?" until you hit the root reason

Root reason vs. immediate reason example:
- Immediate: "Squamous cells are important because they're in the skin"
- Root: "Squamous cells are important because their flat shape is the structural solution to the problem of needing high-surface-area diffusion and protection simultaneously"

The root reason generates a chunk name that is more general, covers more sub-concepts, and creates more lateral connections to other chunks.

Common chunking mistakes:
1. **Prechunking**: using the textbook's headings without evaluating them
2. **Spiderwebbing**: too many connections, no backbone, no evaluation
3. **Waterfalling**: hierarchical only, no lateral connections
4. **Over-detail**: going straight to Layer 4 before Layer 1 is solid

The 2-4 Rule: max 2-4 sub-chunks per chunk. If you have more, your logic layer isn't deep enough.

First principles:
- **Gestalt principles**: the brain naturally groups by similarity, proximity, and common fate. Good chunking works with these tendencies
- **Long-term memory architecture** (Anderson's ACT-R theory): Knowledge stored as propositions and schemas. Schemas with more interconnections are more retrievable

## Retrieval and Interleaving (SIR)

Two types of knowledge:
- **Declarative** (what/why/how): medical knowledge, concepts, mechanisms → Higher-Order first
- **Procedural** (how to execute): math, languages, coding → can't do HO first because formulas needed first

SIR sequence for declarative (after solid encoding):
1. **HO/HV** (High Order, High Volume): Brain dumps, mindmap recalls, teaching → finds what you know
2. **HO/LV** (High Order, Low Volume): Generated questions, practice questions with extended method → finds what you DON'T know
3. **LO** (Lower Order): Flashcards, 3Cs, rote methods → fills arbitrary details

HO/LV is the most neglected and most important for grades. Brain dumps feel productive but only reveal what you already know.

Interleaving rule: don't repeat the same retrieval method twice in a row. Variation is what builds transferable knowledge.

Spacing: always expand gaps. 1 day → 3 days → 1 week → 1 month. The harder the recall, the stronger the encoding.

First principles:
- **Spacing Effect** (Ebbinghaus, 1885): Distributed practice vastly superior to massed practice
- **Interleaving Effect** (Kornell & Bjork, 2008): Mixed practice during retrieval improves discrimination between concepts and transfer
- **Testing Effect / Retrieval Practice** (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006): Active retrieval superior to restudying for long-term retention
- **Hypercorrection Effect** (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001): High-confidence wrong answers that get corrected → stronger encoding than low-confidence corrections. This is why SIR with high-stake questions works

## Traffic Light System (TLS)

Beginner version of AIM-SHOOT for students not yet ready for full BHS.

🔴 Red Light: Generate questions. What am I curious about? Why do I need to know this? What problems does it solve?
🟢 Green Light: Answer those questions. Follow curiosity, don't read linearly. After 2-3 keywords, consolidate and simplify.

Why this works: Forces order control. Prevents passive reading. Creates relevance before detail.

## Cognitive Load (managing the mental effort)

Three types:
- **Intrinsic load**: difficulty of the content itself — can't reduce this
- **Extraneous load**: difficulty caused by bad instruction or format — reduce this (e.g., chunking, mindmaps)
- **Germane load**: mental effort that goes toward schema formation — this is what you want

Good BHS practice optimally manages all three: mindmaps reduce extraneous load while importance-based chunking deliberately applies germane load to the right level (HO).

Optimal cognitive load feels uncomfortable. If studying feels easy, you're probably in lower-order mode.

## Multipass System (Cramming)

For when there's limited time. Swiss-cheese model: multiple passes, each one fills holes from the previous.

Inquiry-led passes (recommended):
- Pass 1: Logic layer only (big picture, ~10% understanding)
- Pass 2: Concepts layer (mechanisms, ~25%)
- Pass 3: Important details (sub-concepts, ~60%)
- Pass 4: Remaining gaps + arbitrary details
- Final pass: Clean sweep against learning objectives

Objective-led passes (when time is critical):
- Each pass covers easiest remaining learning objectives first
- Always adds to a growing GRINDE map

Never start with details, even when cramming.

## Kolb's Experiential Cycle (Reflection)

Used after each study session or exam to drive marginal gains.

1. **Experience**: Name a specific learning situation (process, not event)
2. **Reflection**: What happened? How did you feel? What triggered those reactions?
3. **Abstraction**: What underlying habit, belief, or pattern explains the behavior?
4. **Experiment**: 1-3 small, concrete, testable changes for next time

Key: never stop at symptoms. Always ask "why?" until you hit the root cause. Most learners reflect on what happened (surface). iCanStudy reflection goes to why they acted that way (root).

## Marginal Gains Philosophy

Progress is not binary (success/failure). It's directional.

Three types of gains:
- **Positive**: each gain builds on the last. Compounds.
- **Fluctuating**: gains don't compound. Wrong approach.
- **Negative**: gains compound in wrong direction. Usually from bad technique.

REDO Framework for stacking gains:
- **R**eflect: What might the next 1% improvement look like?
- **E**valuate: What direction?
- **D**efine: Specific next step + time estimate
- **O**ptimise: Concrete plan

## Attention Management (not Time Management)

The highest-level productivity concept in the system.

Time management: blocks time → fills it regardless of efficiency
Attention management: flows from task to task → adjusts based on actual focus, not clock

Flow state is the goal. Time blocks interrupt flow. Attention management maintains it.

Practical: notice when attention drifts → redirect → finish the task → move to the next. No decision delays. No reschedules. Just: attention here, then attention there.

## Common Mistakes Jani Can Self-Diagnose

| Mistake | Symptom | Root Cause |
|---|---|---|
| Prechunking | Map looks like textbook structure | Skipped AIM or rushed it |
| Spiderwebbing | Messy map, can't see backbone | Shooting without evaluating importance |
| Waterfalling | Only top-down arrows, no lateral | Not asking how chunks affect each other |
| Passive reading | Can recall facts but can't apply | No order control, no inquiry |
| Wrong layer order | Overwhelmed by details early | Started with Layer 4, not Layer 1 |
| Under-using HO/LV | Feels prepared but fails exam questions | Over-relying on HO/HV (brain dumps) |
| Missing SKIN | Map is "done" but not actually clean | Stopping after SHOOT |

## Coaching Mode

When coaching Jani on a specific technique:
1. Ask what he did exactly (specific process, not "I studied")
2. Map it to a BHS phase (AIM/SHOOT/SKIN) or a layer (1/2/3/4)
3. Identify the highest-leverage error (usually: wrong layer order, prechunking, or missing HO/LV)
4. Explain the first principle behind the correction (not just "do it differently")
5. Give one concrete experiment to try next session
