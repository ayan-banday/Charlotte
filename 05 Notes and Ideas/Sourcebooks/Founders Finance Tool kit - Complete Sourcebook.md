# CHAPTER 1: How Do I Know Which Numbers to Trust for Decisions?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

You run a 12-person startup called **BuildRight** — you sell project-management software to small construction firms.

Three requests land on your desk the same morning:

1. **Your accountant** sends last year's audited Profit & Loss Account and Balance Sheet.
2. **Your ops lead** asks for budget approval to try a new cloud server setup next quarter.
3. **Your manager (ma'am)** asks you to figure out ROI on buying Claude and Slack for the team.

Three different number requests. Three different types of thinking. If you treat them all the same — or hand everything to the accountant — you'll either move too slowly on internal decisions or look unprepared when a bank or investor asks questions.

Your first job as CEO is not to *prepare* these numbers. It is to know **which kind of accounting answers which question**.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Anil Lamba opens *Romancing the Balance Sheet* with a blunt idea: when someone says *"I'm not a finance person,"* they are really saying *"I don't care what the financial outcome of my actions is."* That is dangerous for a CEO.

Here is the split most founders miss:

| What people think | What is actually true |
|---|---|
| Finance = what accountants do in the back office | **Accounting** records the past; **finance** (financial management) means taking decisions with money consequences |
| Only the finance team needs to understand numbers | Every role — sales, ops, product — makes decisions that hit profit and cash |
| One set of numbers should answer every question | **Two systems** exist because **two audiences** exist: outsiders who need standards, and you who need speed and forecasts |

**Accounting** (in the narrow sense) is clerical: record what happened.  
**Financial management** is intellectual: decide what to do next based on financial impact.

As Lamba puts it: the accountant is the **scorekeeper** in cricket — they record the score. **You and your team are the players.** The scorekeeper does not win the match. Operational decisions win it.

You need both **Financial Accounting (FA)** and **Managerial Accounting (MA)**. Neither replaces the other.

---

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 Why are there two types of accounting?

Businesses face two fundamentally different needs:

**Need 1 — Accountability to the outside world**  
Investors, banks, tax authorities, and regulators ask: *"What did this company do, in a form we can compare and verify?"*  
That requires **rules everyone follows the same way**. That is **Financial Accounting**.

**Need 2 — Running the business day to day**  
You ask: *"Should we launch? What price? Buy this tool? Kill this product line? Can we afford next month's payroll?"*  
That requires **flexible analysis tailored to your decision**. That is **Managerial Accounting**.

Same company. Two lenses. Different questions.

---

### 3.2 Financial Accounting (FA) — the "books"

**What it is:** The standardized recording and reporting of what **already happened**, for **external** users.

**Why it is called "financial accounting":**  
- **Financial** — from Old French *finer*, "to pay, settle." It deals with the money outcomes of the business.  
- **Accounting** — from Latin *computare*, "to count." It is the formal counting and reporting system.

**Orientation:** Past. Last quarter. Last year.

**Audience:** External — banks, investors, regulators, shareholders, tax department.

**Rules:** **GAAP** (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) in the US, **IFRS** (International Financial Reporting Standards) in much of the world including India. These exist so Company A and Company B can be compared fairly.

**Why rules matter:** If every company invented its own format, a bank could not compare your startup to another. Standardization creates **comparability** and **reliability**.

**Timing:** Fixed periods — quarterly, annually — whether you feel like it or not.

**Typical outputs:**
- **Income Statement** (Profit & Loss Account)
- **Balance Sheet**
- **Cash Flow Statement**
- **Statement of Shareholders' Equity**

**What FA is NOT:** A forecast. A budget. A "what if we buy Slack" analysis.

---

### 3.3 Managerial Accounting (MA) — decisions inside the company

**What it is:** Internal analysis — costs, budgets, forecasts, variances — built to help **management decide**.

**Why it is called "managerial":** It is literally *for managers* — people who allocate resources and make choices. Not for the tax department.

**Orientation:** Future. Next quarter. Next product launch.

**Audience:** Internal — you, your leadership team, department heads.

**Rules:** **None mandatory.** No GAAP. No IFRS. You format the report to fit the decision. A cost breakdown for a make-vs-buy choice looks different from a budget variance report.

**Why MA is not governed by GAAP/IFRS:** External standards assume the reader is an outsider who cannot ask follow-up questions. You *can* ask your team anything. You need speed and relevance, not audit-grade uniformity.

**Typical outputs:**
- Cost analysis reports
- Budget vs actual (variance) reports
- Product profitability analysis
- Break-even estimates
- Forecasts and scenarios

**What MA is NOT:** A substitute for audited books. You cannot send your internal budget to a bank and call it financial statements.

---

### 3.4 Side-by-side comparison

| Dimension | Financial Accounting (FA) | Managerial Accounting (MA) |
|---|---|---|
| Time focus | Past | Future |
| Primary users | External (bank, investor, tax) | Internal (you, your team) |
| Rules | GAAP / IFRS — mandatory | Flexible — your choice |
| Report frequency | Fixed periods (quarterly, annual) | Whenever management needs it |
| Purpose | Report and comply | Decide and act |
| Example report | Audited Income Statement | Budget variance report |
| Can include estimates? | Only within accounting rules | Yes — forecasts are the point |

**The line that captures everything:**  
**MA focuses on the future; FA mainly reports past performance.**

---

### 3.5 Why you need both as CEO

| Your question | Use |
|---|---|
| Can we get a bank loan? | FA — they want audited, standardized history |
| Should we buy Slack? | MA — internal cost/benefit, forward-looking |
| Are we profitable? | FA (P&L) — but ask about cash too |
| Why did manufacturing cost exceed budget by 12%? | MA — variance analysis |
| Can an investor compare us to a competitor? | FA — standardized principles |
| Should we discontinue a product line? | MA — contribution and cost per line |

**Using only FA for internal decisions:** Too slow, backward-looking, wrong tool.  
**Using only MA for banks and investors:** They will not accept it. They need audited FA.

---

### 3.6 Related terms you will hear

**Bookkeeping:** The day-to-day recording of transactions. Subset of accounting. Your accountant's team does this.

**Auditing:** Independent checking that FA reports follow the rules. Not the same as either FA or MA — it verifies FA.

**Tax accounting:** How transactions are treated for tax law. Overlaps with FA but has its own rules. Your CA handles filing; you handle whether the business model makes sense.

**Cost accounting:** A branch of MA focused on understanding costs — especially relevant for manufacturing and product businesses. Shows up again in Chapter 3.

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

### The CEO's decision lens

When someone hands you a number or asks for one, run this filter:

```
STEP 1: Who is the audience?
  → External (bank, investor, regulator)     → Financial Accounting
  → Internal (you, your team)                → Managerial Accounting

STEP 2: What time period?
  → Already happened, must be verified       → Financial Accounting
  → Forecast, budget, "what if"              → Managerial Accounting

STEP 3: Is comparability required?
  → Must compare to other companies          → Financial Accounting
  → Specific to your decision only           → Managerial Accounting
```

### Report identification quick guide

| Report | FA or MA? |
|---|---|
| Audited Income Statement | FA |
| Balance Sheet | FA |
| Cash Flow Statement | FA |
| Budget Variance Report | MA |
| Product Cost Analysis | MA |
| Break-even estimate before launch | MA |
| Internal forecast of next year's production costs | MA |
| Last year's profit summary for shareholders | FA |

## 5. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Company:** BuildRight (12 employees, B2B SaaS)

**Request 1 — Bank loan**  
The bank wants two years of audited financial statements.

- **Type:** Financial Accounting  
- **Why:** External user, standardized format, historical performance, credit risk assessment  
- **What you do:** Hand them the audited P&L, Balance Sheet, and Cash Flow your CA prepared. You do not hand them your internal budget.

**Request 2 — Cloud server budget**  
Ops wants ₹40,000/month for new infrastructure next quarter.

- **Type:** Managerial Accounting  
- **Why:** Internal decision, forward-looking, customized to your ops plan  
- **What you do:** Compare budget to expected benefit (uptime, speed, customer churn). This is not a GAAP report — it is a management decision memo.

**Request 3 — Claude + Slack ROI**  
Ma'am asks: total cost, expected benefit, ROI, payback months.

- **Type:** Managerial Accounting  
- **Why:** Internal tool purchase decision, requires estimates and forecasts  
- **What you do:** Build a simple cost-benefit model (Chapter 5 covers this in full).

**Request 4 — VC due diligence**  
A venture capitalist asks for audited financials before term sheet.

- **Type:** Financial Accounting  
- **Why:** External investor, needs standardized, reliable, comparable reports  
- **Not useful to the VC:** Your internal production schedules or employee performance dashboards.

**Two reports on the same CEO's desk:**

| Report | Content | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Report A: Predicts next year's production costs | Forward-looking internal forecast | **Managerial Accounting** |
| Report B: Summarizes last year's profits for shareholders | Historical, external-facing | **Financial Accounting** |

A) Financial Accounting  
B) Managerial Accounting

D) Auditing  

**Answer: B — Managerial Accounting**

# CHAPTER 2: How Do I Tell If My Company Is Actually Making Money?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

**Company:** TableCraft — you buy wooden tables at ₹1,000 each and sell them at ₹1,500 each.

Year 1: You buy 10 tables, sell 6. Your accountant says you made a **loss**. Your gut says you made **profit** — you sold 6 tables at ₹500 margin each, so ₹3,000 profit. Who is right?

Year 2: You sell 4 more tables for ₹6,000 total. Your accountant says profit is only ₹2,000. You expected ₹6,000. What happened?

Meanwhile, your bank statement shows you are **running out of cash** even though the P&L says you are profitable. A supplier calls — payment is overdue. Your investor asks for "the financials." Your ops lead asks if you can afford to hire.

You need three tools — and you need to understand that they answer **different questions**.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Lamba's central warning: **"Never confuse money with profit!"**

Most founders — and many experienced managers — assume:
- Revenue growth = success
- Profit = money in the bank
- What the P&L says = what you can spend

All three assumptions can be wrong.

**Why this matters for you as CEO:**
- You can double revenue and still lose money (rising costs eat the top line).
- You can show profit and still not pay suppliers (cash is stuck in inventory or unpaid invoices).
- You can read financial statements without knowing how to prepare them — Lamba is explicit about this. Your job is judgment, not bookkeeping.

**The primary objective of a business is to earn profit** — but Lamba adds a second pillar: **the ability to manage cash flow.** Winning half the battle (profit) is not enough if you lose the other half (cash).

---

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 Where do financial statements come from?

Every financial transaction — a sale, a salary payment, buying inventory, taking a loan — gets recorded in **accounting records** (the books).

Those records are summarized into two primary statements:

| Statement | Question it answers | Time dimension |
|---|---|---|
| **Profit & Loss Account (P&L)** / **Income Statement** | Did we make or lose money? | **Period** — a month, quarter, year |
| **Balance Sheet** | What do we own and what do we owe? | **Point in time** — a snapshot on one date |

A third statement connects them to cash:

| Statement | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Cash Flow Statement** | Where did cash actually come from and go? |

---

### 3.2 Profit & Loss Account (Income Statement)

**What it is:** A summary of **income** minus **expenses** over a period.

**Why "Profit & Loss":** It shows whether the business made a **profit** (income exceeded expenses) or a **loss** (expenses exceeded income).

**Why "Income Statement":** Same document, different name. "Income" is the accounting word for money earned from operations (primarily sales).

**The basic formula:**

```
Profit = Income − Expenses
Loss   = Expenses − Income    (when expenses are greater)
```

**Top Line vs Bottom Line — why those names?**

On a standard P&L, layout drives the jargon:

| Term | What it means | Where on P&L |
|---|---|---|
| **Top Line** | Total revenue / sales | First major number at the top |
| **Bottom Line** | Net profit after all expenses | Last number at the bottom |

Lamba's warning: *"Seeing the sales figure grow could create a misplaced feeling of success unless it is also accompanied by growing profit."*

Growing the top line without growing the bottom line means you are working harder, not smarter.

**What belongs on the P&L:**
- **Income** — primarily from **sales** (core business). "Other income" exists but sustainable businesses earn from core operations.
- **Expenses** — costs **consumed** in this period (we unpack "consumed" in Chapter 3).

**What does NOT belong on the P&L:**
- Assets you still own (unsold inventory, equipment, cash in bank as a balance)
- Loans you took (that affects Balance Sheet, not P&L directly)

---

### 3.3 The critical distinction: Expenditure vs Expense

This is where most founders get fooled — by their own intuition or by an accountant who does not explain.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Expenditure** | Any money you **spent** — the broadest term |
| **Expense** | The portion of expenditure **consumed** in this period |

**Why this matters:**  
Profit = Income − **Expenses** — NOT Income − **Expenditure**.

If you spend ₹10,00,000 buying tables but only sell half, you did not "expense" ₹10,00,000. Part of that spending is still sitting on your shelf as inventory — an **asset**, not an expense.

Lamba: *"The ultimate test is consumption."*

**The consumption test — two questions:**
1. Was this spending necessary to generate income?
2. Was it **fully consumed** in this period?

- **Yes, fully consumed** → **Expense** → hits P&L, reduces profit  
- **No, not fully consumed** → **Asset** → hits Balance Sheet, does not reduce profit yet

**Depreciation — why this word exists:**  
When you buy equipment that lasts years, you do not expense it all in month one. **Depreciation** is the portion of that asset "used up" each period, recorded as expense. From Latin *de-* (away) + *pretium* (price) — literally writing down the asset's price over time.

---

### 3.4 Balance Sheet

**What it is:** A snapshot of **assets** (what you own) and **liabilities** (what you owe) on a specific date.

**Why "Balance Sheet":**  
In **double-entry bookkeeping**, every transaction affects two accounts equally. Debits always equal credits. So:

```
Assets = Liabilities (+ Owner's Equity)
```

The sheet must **balance**. That is not a suggestion — it is how the system is built.

**Why "Balance":** From the requirement that both sides balance.  
**Why "Sheet":** Historical term for a tabular financial report.

**Lamba's analogy:** *"Financial transactions flow through an organization like water in a river. An instant snapshot of all this financial activity gives the Balance Sheet for that particular moment."*

**Assets — what you own:**
- **Fixed assets** — long-lived (equipment, vehicles, office)
- **Current assets** — short-term (cash, inventory, money customers owe you)

**Why "asset":** From Latin — property with future economic value.

**Liabilities — what you owe:**
- To **owners** (capital invested)
- To **external creditors** (loans, suppliers you have not paid yet)

**Why "liability":** From Latin *ligare* — to bind. Obligations that bind the company.

**Four categories — all accounting data falls into one:**

| Category | Appears on |
|---|---|
| Income | P&L |
| Expenses | P&L |
| Assets | Balance Sheet |
| Liabilities | Balance Sheet |

**Double-entry — why "debit" and "credit" confuse everyone:**

Every transaction hits **two accounts** with equal and opposite effects.

| Term | Origin | In accounting |
|---|---|---|
| **Debit** | Latin *debere* — to owe | One side of a ledger entry |
| **Credit** | Latin *credere* — to believe, trust | The other side |

**Warning for CEOs:** Banking uses "credit" to mean money IN. Accounting uses debit and credit as neutral labels for two sides of an entry. When your accountant says "we credited inventory," they are not saying money arrived — they are describing which side of the ledger entry was affected. You do not need to master debits and credits to read statements. You need to understand **what increased and what decreased**.

**Example — borrow ₹100:**
- Cash (asset) increases by ₹100
- Loan (liability) increases by ₹100  
- Balance Sheet still balances: Assets = Liabilities

---

### 3.5 Cash Flow Statement

**What it is:** Tracks **actual cash** moving in and out over a period.

**Why it exists separately from P&L:**  
The P&L uses **accrual accounting** — you record revenue when **earned**, expenses when **incurred**, even if cash has not moved yet.

**Examples of profit vs cash disconnect:**
- You sold ₹5,00,000 on credit — revenue on P&L, no cash yet
- You bought ₹3,00,000 of inventory in cash — cash out, but unsold stock is an asset not fully expensed
- You show depreciation expense on P&L — reduces profit, but no cash left the bank

Lamba: *"Only half the battle has been won when you are able to make profit. The other half will be won if you are also able to generate cash."*

**Profits do not represent money.** A profitable company can have a **negative bank balance**.

---

### 3.6 How the three statements connect

```
ACCOUNTING RECORDS (every transaction)
        │
        ├──► P&L (period):     Income − Expenses = Profit
        │
        ├──► Balance Sheet (snapshot):  Assets = Liabilities
        │
        └──► Cash Flow (period):  Actual cash in − cash out

CONSUMPTION TEST links P&L and Balance Sheet:
  Unconsumed spending → Asset (Balance Sheet)
  Consumed spending   → Expense (P&L)
```

**CEO reading order when something feels wrong:**
1. **P&L** — Are we profitable on paper?
2. **Cash Flow** — Is cash actually moving?
3. **Balance Sheet** — Where is value stuck? (Inventory? Receivables? Debt?)

---

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

### Reading a P&L — what to look for

```
REVENUE (Top Line)                    ₹XX,XX,XXX
− Cost of goods sold / operating costs  ₹XX,XX,XXX
= GROSS PROFIT                          ₹XX,XX,XXX
− Operating expenses (rent, salaries)   ₹XX,XX,XXX
= OPERATING PROFIT                      ₹XX,XX,XXX
− Interest, taxes, other                ₹XX,XX,XXX
= NET PROFIT (Bottom Line)              ₹XX,XX,XXX
```

**Three questions to ask:**
1. Is revenue growing?
2. Is the bottom line growing **with** revenue?
3. Where are the biggest expense lines — and are they necessary?

### Reading a Balance Sheet — what to look for

```
ASSETS                          LIABILITIES
Fixed assets (equipment)        Owner's capital
Current assets:                 Long-term loans
  Cash                          Current liabilities:
  Inventory                       Supplier dues
  Receivables                     Short-term debt
```

**Three questions to ask:**
1. How much cash do we actually have?
2. How much is locked in inventory or unpaid invoices?
3. How much do we owe, and when is it due?

### Reading Cash Flow — what to look for

Three sections:
- **Operating** — cash from running the business
- **Investing** — cash spent on or received from assets
- **Financing** — loans, equity, repayments

**The one question:** Is operating cash flow positive? If P&L shows profit but operating cash flow is negative, find out where cash is trapped.

## 5. WORKED EXAMPLE

### TableCraft — Year 1 (the profit puzzle)

**Unit economics:**
- Buy price: ₹1,000 per table
- Sell price: ₹1,500 per table
- Margin per table sold: ₹500

**Activity:**
- Purchased: 10 tables → ₹10,000 cash out
- Sold: 6 tables → ₹9,000 revenue
- Unsold: 4 tables still in stock

**Wrong method (treating all purchases as expense):**

| | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Income | 9,000 |
| Expense (all 10 tables purchased) | 10,000 |
| **Result** | **LOSS of 1,000** |

**Correct method (only consumed goods are expense):**

| | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Income | 9,000 |
| Cost of goods sold (6 tables × ₹1,000) | 6,000 |
| **Profit** | **3,000** |
| Unsold inventory (asset on Balance Sheet) | 4,000 |

Your gut was right. The accountant's "loss" was a **classification error** — expensing inventory you still own.

---

### TableCraft — Year 2 (opening inventory)

**Activity:**
- Opening inventory: 4 tables worth ₹4,000 (carried from Year 1)
- Sold: 4 tables for ₹6,000 (4 × ₹1,500)
- No new purchases

| | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Revenue | 6,000 |
| Cost of goods sold (4 tables consumed from opening stock) | 4,000 |
| **Profit** | **2,000** |

You received ₹6,000 cash but profit is only ₹2,000 — because ₹4,000 of that revenue replaces the cost of tables you already paid for in Year 1. **Profit follows consumption, not cash received.**

---

### TableCraft — the cash trap (furniture store version)

Lamba's larger example:

| Event | Amount |
|---|---|
| Buy 100 tables at ₹1,000 | ₹1,00,000 cash OUT |
| Sell 50 tables at ₹1,500 | ₹75,000 cash IN |
| Unsold inventory (50 tables) | ₹50,000 locked in stock |

**P&L may show profit** (revenue ₹75,000 minus COGS on 50 units ₹50,000 = ₹25,000 profit).

**Bank reality:** You spent ₹1,00,000 and received ₹75,000. You are **₹25,000 poorer in cash** and still holding ₹50,000 of unsold stock.

**This is the "profitable but broke" problem.**

---

### Balance Sheet snapshot — two companies, same assets

Lenders look at structure, not just profit:

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Share Capital (owner money) | 450 | 150 |
| Long-term Loans | 200 | 650 |
| Current Liabilities | 50 | 100 |
| **Total Liabilities** | **700** | **900** |
| Fixed Assets | 750 | 750 |
| Current Assets | 250 | 250 |
| **Total Assets** | **1000** | **1000** |
| Owner contribution % | **75%** | **15%** |

Same total assets. Company A is mostly owner-funded. Company B is mostly debt-funded. **A bank prefers A** — more owner skin in the game, lower lender risk.

# CHAPTER 3: How Do I Calculate What Something Really Costs?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

**Company:** PackRight — you manufacture custom shipping boxes for e-commerce brands.

Three decisions land this week:

1. **Make vs buy:** A vendor offers to manufacture boxes at ₹12/unit. Your ops team says in-house cost is "around ₹10." Your accountant's P&L shows total manufacturing costs of ₹8,00,000 last month. Which number do you trust?

2. **Pricing:** A big client wants 50,000 boxes at ₹14/unit. Your usual price is ₹18. Your sales head says "revenue is revenue." You are not sure.

3. **Cash crunch:** You are profitable on paper but payroll is tight. Your CA says costs are "under control." You do not believe it.

All three require the same foundation: **knowing what something really costs** — not the sticker price, not the bank outflow, not the P&L total blindly.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Founders routinely confuse four different "costs":

| What people say | What they often mean | What you actually need |
|---|---|---|
| "We spent ₹X" | Cash that left the bank | Cash cost |
| "It's on the P&L" | Expense recognized this period | Consumed cost |
| "Fully loaded cost" | Everything allocated to a product | Full cost (for pricing long-term) |
| "Cost of one more unit" | — | Marginal / variable cost (for specific decisions) |

Using the wrong cost for the wrong decision is how businesses **lose money while growing revenue**.

Lamba's line worth tattooing on your brain: **"It is consumption and not spending which should be considered as an expense."**

And for decisions: **"Profit is an opinion; cash in the bank is a fact."**

---

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 The three layers of "true cost"

As CEO, you need three layers — each answers a different question:

| Layer | Question | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Cash cost** | What left the bank? | Bank statement, cash flow |
| **2. Consumed cost** | What was used up to earn this period's revenue? | P&L, COGS, consumption test |
| **3. Decision cost** | What changes if we do X vs Y? | Variable cost, contribution, break-even |

Chapter 2 covered layers 1 and 2. This chapter builds layer 3 — and connects all three.

---

### 3.2 Fixed costs vs variable costs — why the distinction exists

Not all costs behave the same when you sell more or less.

| Type | Behavior | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Variable costs (VC)** | Rise and fall **with volume** | Raw materials per box, packaging per unit, payment processing %, sales commission per sale |
| **Fixed costs (FC)** | Stay the same **in short run** regardless of volume | Rent, core salaried team, insurance, software subscriptions |

**Why "variable":** They **vary** with output.  
**Why "fixed":** They are **fixed** in place for the period — you pay rent whether you sell 100 boxes or 10,000.

**Semi-variable costs:** Utilities, overtime — part fixed, part variable. For decisions, split them as best you can.

**Why this matters for startups:**  
- High fixed costs = high upside when sales grow, **high danger** when sales drop.  
- Early-stage companies often prefer **variable** costs (freelancers, cloud, commission) until demand is predictable.  
- Every hire, lease, or equipment purchase **raises your break-even** — the sales you need just to stop losing money.

---

### 3.3 Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) — why "sold" is in the name

**COGS** = the cost of inventory **consumed** (sold or used) in the period.

**Formula:**

```
COGS = Opening Inventory + Purchases − Closing Inventory
```

**Why not just "purchases"?**  
Because buying stock is not the same as using it. Unsold inventory is an asset, not yet a cost of sales.

**TableCraft recap:**

| Year | Purchases | Closing stock | COGS | Logic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₹10,000 (10 tables) | ₹4,000 (4 unsold) | ₹6,000 | Only 6 sold |
| 2 | ₹0 | ₹0 | ₹4,000 | Used opening stock |

---

### 3.4 Contribution — why this word?

Once you separate variable from fixed costs, a new number appears:

```
Contribution = Sales − Variable Costs
```

**Why "contribution"?**  
Lamba: *"Contribution is called Contribution because it is the contribution of each unit sold towards the organization's bottom line."*

Each sale **contributes** first toward covering fixed costs. Only after fixed costs are covered does each sale add **profit**.

```
Profit = Contribution − Fixed Costs
```

**Why this is more useful than "profit" alone for decisions:**  
Contribution tells you whether **one more sale** helps or hurts — regardless of how you allocate fixed costs across products.

---

### 3.5 Full cost vs marginal cost — two different questions

| Concept | Question | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **Full cost** | What does it cost to make and sustain this product? | Long-term pricing, profitability analysis |
| **Marginal (variable) cost** | What does **one more unit** cost? | Accept/reject an order, make vs buy, discount decisions |

**Critical insight from Lamba:**  
You can rationally sell **below full cost** but **above variable cost** if fixed costs are already committed for the period. You still **lose money overall**, but you lose **less** than shutting down — because each sale contributes something toward fixed costs.

**Wrong:** "Price must cover full cost per unit on every order."  
**Right:** "Does this price exceed variable cost and serve a strategic purpose?"

---

### 3.6 Make vs buy — what "cost" means in this decision

Outsourcing vs in-house is not answered by the Income Statement total. You need a **Cost Analysis Report** — a MA tool that compares:

| Cost element | In-house | Outsource |
|---|---|---|
| Direct materials per unit | ? | Included in vendor price |
| Direct labor per unit | ? | — |
| Allocated overhead | ? | — |
| Vendor price per unit | — | ? |
| Quality control / shipping | ? | ? |
| Flexibility / lead time | Qualitative | Qualitative |

The P&L shows **total** manufacturing cost. It does not automatically split what changes if you outsource.

---

### 3.7 Inventory — the cost trap that fakes profit

**Why inventory distorts cost:**

Accountants often record **purchases when goods are bought** (practical bookkeeping). Economically, only **sold/consumed** goods are this period's cost.

**The danger:**  
Higher closing inventory → lower recognized expense → **higher reported profit** — while **cash already left** to buy stock.

**Royal Dutch Shell** (mentioned by Lamba) inflated inventory values to overstate profit. You may not commit fraud — but you can fool yourself by bulk-buying inventory and feeling profitable.

**CEO rule:** Ask not just "what's profit?" but **"how much cash is sitting in inventory?"**

---

### 3.8 Working capital — the cost of running day to day

**Working capital** = money tied up in daily operations.

```
Gross Working Capital  = Current Assets (cash + inventory + receivables)
Net Working Capital    = Current Assets − Current Liabilities
```

**Why it matters for cost:**  
Supplier credit reduces immediate cash need — but if you rely too heavily on short-term debt to fund operations, your **current ratio** falls and banks see risk.

**Mini example (Lamba):**

| Item | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Fixed Assets | 80 |
| Current Assets | 20 |
| Current Liabilities | 10 |
| **Net Working Capital** | **10** |

You need ₹10 of long-term funding to sustain day-to-day operations after supplier credit.

**Cost you feel:** If ₹15 of your cash is in slow-moving inventory, that is ₹15 not available for payroll — even if P&L looks fine.

---

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

### Framework 1 — Consumption test (from Ch 2, applied to cost)

```
Money spent
    │
    ├── Fully consumed this period?  → Expense / COGS  → reduces profit
    │
    └── Not fully consumed?        → Asset (inventory, equipment)  → Balance Sheet
                                          │
                                          └── Used over time?  → Depreciation expense each period
```

### Framework 2 — Contribution and break-even

```
Contribution per unit = Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit

Break-even units = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit

Break-even sales (₹) = Break-even units × Selling Price
```

**Break-even** = the point where Contribution exactly covers Fixed Costs. No profit, no loss. Named because you "break even" — you stop bleeding.

**Profit at any volume:**

```
Profit = (Units sold × Contribution per unit) − Fixed Costs
```

**PV Ratio (Profit-Volume Ratio):**

```
PV Ratio = Contribution ÷ Sales
```

Shows what percentage of each rupee of sales becomes contribution. Useful for comparing product lines.

### Framework 3 — Decision checklist

| Decision | Cost measure to use |
|---|---|
| Monthly profitability | P&L with correct COGS (consumed cost) |
| Can we pay bills? | Cash flow + inventory + receivables |
| Accept discount bulk order? | Contribution per unit (variable cost vs offered price) |
| Outsource production? | Cost Analysis Report (full comparison) |
| What must we sell to survive? | Break-even units |
| Is growth helping? | Inventory days + net working capital trend |

### PackRight — Unit economics and break-even

**Given:**
- Selling price per box: ₹18
- Variable cost per box (materials + direct labor + shipping): ₹11
- Fixed costs per month (rent, salaried staff, machinery depreciation): ₹2,80,000

**Step 1 — Contribution per unit**

```
Contribution = ₹18 − ₹11 = ₹7 per box
```

**Step 2 — Break-even volume**

```
Break-even units = ₹2,80,000 ÷ ₹7 = 40,000 boxes/month
```

PackRight must sell **40,000 boxes** just to cover fixed costs.

**Step 3 — Profit at 55,000 boxes**

```
Total contribution = 55,000 × ₹7 = ₹3,85,000
Profit = ₹3,85,000 − ₹2,80,000 = ₹1,05,000
```

**Step 4 — The discount order decision**

Big client offers 50,000 boxes at **₹14/unit** (below your ₹18 price).

| | Per unit |
|---|---|
| Offered price | ₹14 |
| Variable cost | ₹11 |
| **Contribution** | **₹3** |

Full cost per box (if you allocate fixed costs): ₹11 + (₹2,80,000 ÷ current volume). At 40,000 units, fixed allocation = ₹7/box → "full cost" ≈ ₹18.

**Analysis:**  
- ₹14 is **below full cost** — looks like a loss.  
- ₹14 is **above variable cost** (₹11) — each box still contributes ₹3 toward fixed costs.  
- 50,000 × ₹3 = ₹1,50,000 contribution from this order.

**If fixed costs are already committed this month:** Accepting may be rational — you are better off contributing ₹1,50,000 toward rent and salaries than leaving capacity idle.

**If this becomes your only customer at ₹14:** You renegotiate or restructure fixed costs — long-term pricing must cover full cost.

# CHAPTER 4: How Do I Know When I Stop Losing Money?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

**Company:** **MasalaBox Kitchen** — a cloud kitchen in Bengaluru. You cook North Indian meals and sell through Swiggy and Zomato. No dine-in. One kitchen, four cooks, two delivery riders on payroll.

It is month six. Revenue is climbing — ₹12 lakh last month, up from ₹8 lakh three months ago. Your accountant says you are "almost profitable." Your bank balance says otherwise. Rent is due in ten days and you are ₹1.8 lakh short.

Your ops lead walks in with three questions:

1. **"How many orders do we actually need to stop losing money?"**
2. **"Swiggy wants us on a 30% discount campaign. Should we join?"**
3. **"If we want ₹50,000 profit next month, how many orders is that?"**

You cannot answer any of these from the audited P&L alone. The P&L tells you what happened last month. These questions are about **what must happen next** — and that requires a different tool: **break-even analysis**, built on **marginal costing**.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Startups do not fail because founders cannot read a Balance Sheet. They fail because founders **grow revenue while still losing money on every order** — or because they accept discount deals that look like "growth" but destroy contribution.

Three traps MasalaBox is facing right now:

| Trap | What it sounds like | What is actually happening |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue vanity** | "We did ₹12 lakh in sales!" | Sales ≠ profit. Fixed costs may still exceed contribution. |
| **Full-cost pricing panic** | "₹180 per meal costs us ₹220 to make — every order loses ₹40!" | Full cost includes fixed costs allocated per unit. That is wrong for a one-off discount decision. |
| **Cash vs break-even confusion** | "We broke even on the P&L but cannot pay rent" | Break-even is a **contribution** concept. Cash timing (commissions paid weekly, rent monthly) is separate. |

Lamba's warning applies directly: **"Profit is an opinion; cash in the bank is a fact."** Break-even analysis tells you when contribution covers fixed costs — your **minimum survival sales level**. It does not replace cash flow planning, but without it you are flying blind on pricing, discounts, and growth.

This is **Managerial Accounting** at its most useful: a forward-looking calculation you run in a spreadsheet, not a report your CA files with the tax department.

---

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 Marginal costing — why "marginal"?

**Marginal costing** (also called **variable costing** for decision purposes) focuses on what changes when you sell **one more unit** — the **margin** at the edge of your business.

- **Marginal** — from Latin *margo*, "edge." The cost or revenue at the **margin** of your current activity: one more thali, one more biryani order.
- **Costing** — assigning costs to understand what you are really spending.

Under marginal costing, costs split into two buckets:

| Bucket | Definition | MasalaBox examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Variable costs (VC)** | Change with each order | Ingredients, packaging, aggregator commission %, per-order delivery fee, payment gateway charges |
| **Fixed costs (FC)** | Stay the same for the period regardless of orders | Kitchen rent, core cook salaries, insurance, base software subscriptions |

**Why separate them?** Because fixed costs are **already committed** whether you sell 50 orders or 500. The decision question is never "does this order cover rent?" — rent is sunk for the month. The question is: **"Does this order contribute something toward rent, or does it cost us more than it brings in?"**

That "something" has a name.

---

### 3.2 Contribution — the building block

You met contribution in Chapter 3. Here is why it sits at the centre of break-even analysis:

```
Contribution = Sales − Variable Costs
```

Or per unit:

```
Contribution per unit = Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit
```

**Why "contribution"?** Lamba: *"Contribution is called Contribution because it is the contribution of each unit sold towards the organization's bottom line."*

Think of fixed costs as a **hurdle**. Every order throws contribution over the hurdle. Until total contribution equals fixed costs, you are **below break-even** — still losing money overall. After that point, each order adds **profit**.

```
Profit = Total Contribution − Fixed Costs
```

**Key insight:** A sale can have **positive contribution** but the business still loses money — if total contribution has not yet cleared fixed costs. You are losing **less** with each sale, not necessarily making profit yet.

---

### 3.3 Break-even — why "break even"?

**Break-even** is the sales volume where total contribution **exactly equals** fixed costs. No profit. No loss. You "break even" — you stop the bleeding.

At break-even:

```
Total Contribution = Fixed Costs
```

**Why the term matters psychologically:** Founders often celebrate "record revenue months" without knowing their break-even point. If break-even is ₹14 lakh and you did ₹12 lakh, you had your best month ever — and still lost money. The break-even number turns vague anxiety into a **concrete target**.

Two ways to express it:

| Measure | Formula | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Break-even units** | Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit | How many orders you need |
| **Break-even sales (₹)** | Break-even units × Selling Price | Revenue value of that volume |

---

### 3.4 PV Ratio — why "profit-volume"?

The **PV Ratio** (Profit-Volume Ratio), also called **Contribution Margin Ratio**:

```
PV Ratio = Contribution ÷ Sales × 100
```

Or per unit:

```
PV Ratio = Contribution per unit ÷ Selling Price × 100
```

**Why "profit-volume"?** It shows the relationship between **volume of sales** and **contribution toward profit**. It answers: *"Of every ₹100 of revenue, how many rupees are available to cover fixed costs and profit?"*

**Example intuition:** If PV Ratio is 40%, every ₹1,00,000 of sales contributes ₹40,000 toward fixed costs. Once fixed costs are covered, that same 40% flows to profit.

**Why it is useful:** Compare product lines or channels quickly. A biryani with 50% PV Ratio is more "efficient" at generating contribution than a ₹99 combo at 20% — even if the combo sells more units.

---

### 3.5 Target profit — extending break-even

Break-even is profit = zero. But Priya wants **₹50,000 profit**. The formula extends naturally:

```
Required Contribution = Fixed Costs + Target Profit

Break-even units (for target profit) = (Fixed Costs + Target Profit) ÷ Contribution per unit
```

**Why this matters:** Investors, lenders, and your own salary are not optional forever. "Stop losing money" is step one. "Earn enough to pay myself" is step two. Target profit calculation turns break-even into a **business plan number**.

---

### 3.6 Operating leverage — a first look

**Operating leverage** measures how sensitive your profit is to changes in sales volume, given your mix of fixed and variable costs.

```
Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL) = Contribution ÷ Profit
```

(At a given volume, when profit > 0.)

**Why "leverage"?** Fixed costs act like a **lever**. Once you clear break-even, each additional rupee of sales drops mostly to profit — because variable costs are the only thing rising. High fixed costs mean:

- **Above break-even:** Profits grow fast. A 10% sales increase might produce a 30% profit increase.
- **Below break-even:** Losses are painful. A 10% sales drop deepens the hole because fixed costs do not shrink.

**Why startups should care:** Cloud kitchens, SaaS, and manufacturing all share high fixed costs. Lamba's point: understand your leverage before you celebrate growth. **Revenue up 20% means nothing if you are still below break-even.**

---

### 3.7 The discount decision — marginal costing in action

A discount campaign (Swiggy's 30% off, a bulk corporate order, a festival offer) triggers one question:

**Is the discounted price still above variable cost per unit?**

| Scenario | Decision logic |
|---|---|
| Price > Variable Cost | **Positive contribution.** May accept if capacity is idle and fixed costs are already committed. |
| Price = Variable Cost | **Zero contribution.** You are working for free to cover nothing. Only accept for strategic reasons (customer acquisition with known lifetime value). |
| Price < Variable Cost | **Negative contribution.** Every order **increases** your loss. Reject unless it triggers profitable follow-on business you can measure. |

**Why founders get this wrong:** They compare the discount price to **full cost** (variable + allocated fixed). Full cost is for **long-term pricing**. Variable cost is for **this-order decisions**.

Lamba's logic: selling below full cost but above variable cost does not mean you are "profitable" — it means you are **minimising loss** when fixed costs are already unavoidable.

---

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

### Framework — The break-even toolkit

**Step 1: Separate variable and fixed costs for the period (usually one month)**

List every cost. Ask: *"If we sold zero orders this month, would we still pay this?"* Yes → Fixed. No → Variable.

**Step 2: Calculate contribution per unit**

```
Contribution per unit = Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit
```

Use **net selling price** if aggregator commission comes out of revenue (price minus platform fee).

**Step 3: Calculate break-even volume**

```
Break-even units = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit
```

Round **up** — you cannot sell 847.3 thalis.

**Step 4: Calculate break-even sales value**

```
Break-even sales (₹) = Break-even units × Selling Price
```

**Step 5: Calculate profit at any given volume**

```
Profit = (Units sold × Contribution per unit) − Fixed Costs
```

**Step 6: Calculate volume needed for target profit**

```
Required units = (Fixed Costs + Target Profit) ÷ Contribution per unit
```

**Step 7: PV Ratio (for comparison across products)**

```
PV Ratio = Contribution per unit ÷ Selling Price × 100
```

### Quick reference — all formulas

| What you need | Formula |
|---|---|
| Contribution per unit | SP − VC per unit |
| Total contribution | Units × Contribution per unit |
| Break-even units | FC ÷ Contribution per unit |
| Break-even sales (₹) | Break-even units × SP |
| Profit | Total contribution − FC |
| Units for target profit | (FC + Target profit) ÷ Contribution per unit |
| PV Ratio | Contribution ÷ Sales × 100 |
| Operating leverage | Contribution ÷ Profit (at current volume) |

### Decision checklist — when to use what

| Question | Tool |
|---|---|
| How many orders to survive this month? | Break-even units |
| What revenue number must we hit? | Break-even sales (₹) |
| Are we making money at current volume? | Profit formula |
| How many orders for ₹X profit? | Target profit formula |
| Should we accept this discount? | Contribution per unit at discounted price vs variable cost |
| Which dish is better for the business? | PV Ratio comparison |

## 5. WORKED EXAMPLE

### MasalaBox Kitchen — November data

**Priya pulls one month of clean numbers:**

| Item | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Average selling price per order (customer pays) | 250 |
| Aggregator commission (22% of selling price) | 55 |
| **Net revenue per order** (what MasalaBox receives) | **195** |
| Ingredients + packaging per order | 85 |
| Per-order delivery rider cost (variable portion) | 25 |
| Payment gateway / misc per order | 5 |
| **Total variable cost per order** | **115** |
| Monthly kitchen rent | 1,20,000 |
| Cook and staff salaries (fixed) | 2,40,000 |
| Insurance, utilities (fixed), software | 40,000 |
| **Total fixed costs per month** | **4,00,000** |

---

**Step 1 — Contribution per order**

Use **net revenue** (after commission) minus variable cost:

```
Contribution per order = ₹195 − ₹115 = ₹80
```

Each order contributes ₹80 toward covering rent, salaries, and profit.

---

**Step 2 — PV Ratio**

```
PV Ratio = ₹80 ÷ ₹195 × 100 = 41.0%
```

Roughly 41 paise of every rupee received (after commission) goes to fixed costs and profit.

---

**Step 3 — Break-even units**

```
Break-even orders = ₹4,00,000 ÷ ₹80 = 5,000 orders/month
```

MasalaBox must fulfil **5,000 orders** just to stop losing money.

At ~167 orders/day in a 30-day month. Priya now has a concrete target, not a feeling.

---

**Step 4 — Break-even sales value**

Two ways to express it:

**Gross sales (what customers pay):**

```
5,000 orders × ₹250 = ₹12,50,000/month
```

**Net sales (what MasalaBox receives after commission):**

```
5,000 orders × ₹195 = ₹9,75,000/month
```

When talking to your team, use **gross order count and customer-facing price** — that is what ops tracks. When checking against bank inflows, use **net**.

---

**Step 5 — Profit at 6,200 orders (last month's actual volume)**

Priya thought 6,200 orders meant a great month. Let's check:

```
Total contribution = 6,200 × ₹80 = ₹4,96,000
Profit = ₹4,96,000 − ₹4,00,000 = ₹96,000
```

**Result:** MasalaBox earned **₹96,000 profit** — above break-even by 1,200 orders. Good month. But Priya was ₹1.8 lakh short on rent — because **profit ≠ cash in hand** (commissions, supplier payments, and last month's losses consumed cash). Break-even told her the business model works at this volume; cash flow tells her **when** she can pay rent.

---

**Step 6 — Target profit: ₹50,000 next month**

```
Required contribution = ₹4,00,000 + ₹50,000 = ₹4,50,000

Required orders = ₹4,50,000 ÷ ₹80 = 5,625 orders
```

To earn **₹50,000 profit**, MasalaBox needs **5,625 orders** — 625 more than break-even.

**Check:**

```
5,625 × ₹80 = ₹4,50,000 contribution
₹4,50,000 − ₹4,00,000 = ₹50,000 profit ✓
```

---

**Step 7 — The Swiggy 30% discount campaign**

Swiggy proposes: MasalaBox offers 30% off to customers. Customer pays ₹175 instead of ₹250. Commission stays 22%.

| | Normal order | Discount order |
|---|---|---|
| Customer pays | ₹250 | ₹175 |
| Commission (22%) | ₹55 | ₹38.50 |
| **Net to MasalaBox** | **₹195** | **₹136.50** |
| Variable cost | ₹115 | ₹115 |
| **Contribution** | **₹80** | **₹21.50** |

**Analysis:**

- Discount price (₹136.50 net) is **above** variable cost (₹115). Contribution is **positive** — ₹21.50 per order.
- Discount price is **below** normal contribution. Each discount order contributes **₹58.50 less** than a full-price order.
- Full cost per order (allocating fixed costs at 5,000 break-even volume): ₹115 + (₹4,00,000 ÷ 5,000) = ₹115 + ₹80 = ₹195. At ₹136.50 net, discount orders look like a "loss" on full-cost basis — **misleading for this decision**.

**Break-even impact if ALL sales were discount orders:**

```
₹4,00,000 ÷ ₹21.50 = 18,605 orders needed to break even
```

vs 5,000 at full price. The campaign **raises break-even by 3.7×** if it becomes your normal price.

**Priya's decision framework:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Is contribution positive? | Yes — ₹21.50/order |
| Do we have idle kitchen capacity? | Yes — can cook more without new fixed cost |
| Will discount customers only ever order at ₹175? | Unknown — risky if yes |
| Does Swiggy guarantee minimum order volume? | Ask for 2,000+ incremental orders |

**Decision:** Accept **only** if incremental discount orders exceed the volume needed to offset lost contribution on full-price orders. If the campaign brings 3,000 **new** discount orders: 3,000 × ₹21.50 = ₹64,500 extra contribution. That helps — but only if those customers would not have ordered at ₹250 anyway.

**Rule:** Never accept a discount that pushes price **below variable cost**. This one does not — but it moves break-even sharply upward.

---

**Step 8 — Operating leverage snapshot**

At 6,200 orders with ₹96,000 profit:

```
DOL = Contribution ÷ Profit = ₹4,96,000 ÷ ₹96,000 ≈ 5.2
```

A 10% increase in orders (to 6,820) adds roughly ₹40,000 contribution (620 × ₹80). Profit rises from ₹96,000 to ~₹1,36,000 — a **42% profit increase** from 10% more volume. That is operating leverage working in your favour — **above break-even**.

Below break-even, the same leverage works against you.

# CHAPTER 5: How Do I Justify a Purchase and Figure Out ROI?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

Your startup has twelve people. Everyone is on WhatsApp for work, Google Docs for drafts, and email for anything that falls between. It works, but it does not scale. Messages get lost. Someone asks the same question three times in a week. A founder spends forty-five minutes rewriting a client email that could have taken ten.

You propose two tools: **Claude** for drafting, summarising, and research, and **Slack** for team communication. The combined cost is not trivial—subscriptions for twelve people, plus a one-time setup fee to migrate channels, train the team, and wire up integrations.

Your co-founder—let's call her Ma'am, because that is how half the startup ecosystem refers to the person who actually reads the numbers—asks a fair question:

> "Why are we spending this much? What is the ROI?"

She is not being difficult. She is doing her job. Every rupee in a twelve-person company is a rupee that could pay salary, extend runway, or fund a customer pilot. "Everyone uses Slack" is not a financial argument. "This saves us X hours per week, which is worth Y rupees, and we break even in Z months" is.

This chapter teaches you how to build that argument—and how to spot when a purchase does not deserve one.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Startups do not fail because founders bought the wrong SaaS tool. They fail because they ran out of money, or because they spent money on things that did not move the business forward.

Purchase justification is not bureaucracy. It is a habit of connecting spending to outcomes. When you justify a purchase properly, you get three things:

1. **Clarity for yourself.** You know whether you are buying productivity, revenue, or peace of mind—and whether the price matches.
2. **Credibility with investors and co-founders.** Finance people respect numbers, even rough ones. "I think it'll help" earns a nod. "Payback in nine months on loaded labour cost" earns a budget line.
3. **A decision framework for later.** When the next tool pitches you—CRM, analytics, AI copilot—you already know how to run the same math instead of debating vibes.

The goal is not to prove every purchase is worth it. Sometimes the honest answer is "no, not yet." That is also a good outcome.

---

## 3. THE THEORY

### Financial modeling for CEOs (the minimum you need)

You do not need an MBA spreadsheet. You need a simple model with four boxes:

| Box | Question it answers |
|-----|---------------------|
| **Costs** | What do we pay, when, and for how long? |
| **Benefits** | What money comes in, or what cost goes away? |
| **Timeline** | Over what period are we measuring? |
| **Assumptions** | What has to be true for this to work? |

Write the assumptions down. They are the part people argue about—and the part you must defend.

### ROI (Return on Investment)

ROI tells you how much you gained relative to what you spent.

```
ROI (%) = (Net Benefit ÷ Total Investment) × 100
```

Where:

- **Total Investment** = all costs (subscriptions, setup, training, migration time if you choose to count it)
- **Net Benefit** = Total measurable benefits minus Total Investment

**Example:** You invest ₹100,000 and generate ₹150,000 in measurable benefit. Net benefit = ₹50,000. ROI = 50%.

ROI is useful for comparing options. It is less useful when benefits are hard to measure (brand, morale, risk reduction). Do not fake precision on soft benefits—label them separately.

### Payback period

Payback answers a simpler question: **How long until this purchase has paid for itself?**

```
Payback (months) = Total Investment ÷ Monthly Net Benefit
```

Where **Monthly Net Benefit** = monthly measurable benefits minus monthly recurring costs.

Payback is what Ma'am often cares about more than ROI. A 200% ROI that takes four years to realise is worse for a startup than a 40% ROI that pays back in six months—because runway is measured in months, not ratios.

### Jargon, translated

| Term | Plain English |
|------|---------------|
| **Capex** | One-time spend (setup, hardware, implementation) |
| **Opex** | Ongoing spend (monthly subscriptions) |
| **Fully loaded cost** | Salary plus taxes, benefits, office, tools—what an employee actually costs per hour |
| **Hard benefit** | Rupees you can point to (revenue, cancelled subscription, fewer contractors) |
| **Soft benefit** | Real but fuzzy (faster decisions, fewer mistakes, happier team) |
| **Run rate** | What a cost or benefit looks like if sustained for a full year |
| **Sensitivity** | What happens if your assumptions are wrong—e.g. half the time savings |

When someone says "we need ROI," they usually mean: **show me payback on hard benefits, and tell me what assumptions you're betting on.**

---

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

Here is a repeatable five-step process. Use it for any tool, hire, or vendor.

### Step 1: List all costs (don't forget the hidden ones)

- Per-user subscription × users × months
- One-time setup (vendor fees, your engineer's time, migration)
- Training time (optional but honest—two hours × twelve people is twenty-four hours)
- Cancellation costs of old tools (if any lock-in)

Separate **Year 1** (includes one-time costs) from **Year 2+** (recurring only).

### Step 2: List measurable benefits

Ask: **What changes in rupees if this works?**

Common categories for productivity tools:

- **Time saved** → convert hours to rupees using fully loaded hourly cost
- **Revenue enabled** → faster sales cycle, more proposals sent, better conversion
- **Cost avoided** → cancelled tool, fewer support tickets, less contractor spend
- **Error reduction** → fewer refunds, fewer compliance fixes

If you cannot quantify it, put it in a "soft benefits" list. Do not smuggle it into the ROI number.

### Step 3: Convert time to money

This is where most startup ROI models live or die.

```
Hourly loaded cost = Monthly fully loaded salary ÷ Working hours per month
```

A common shortcut for Indian startups: if average fully loaded cost is ₹60,000/month and productive hours are ~150/month, hourly cost ≈ **₹400/hour**. Adjust for your team.

### Step 4: Calculate ROI and payback

Use Year 1 numbers for a conservative picture (because setup hits once). Show Year 2 ROI separately if recurring benefits continue but setup does not repeat.

### Step 5: Stress-test assumptions

Run three scenarios:

| Scenario | Time saved assumption |
|----------|----------------------|
| Conservative | 50% of your estimate |
| Base | Your honest estimate |
| Optimistic | 125% of your estimate |

If ROI is only positive in the optimistic case, the purchase is a bet—not a justified investment.

## 5. WORKED EXAMPLE: Claude + Slack for a 12-Person Startup

### The proposal

| Item | Rate | Calculation | Annual cost |
|------|------|-------------|-------------|
| Claude (per user) | ₹2,000/month | ₹2,000 × 12 users × 12 months | **₹288,000** |
| Slack (per user) | ₹800/month | ₹800 × 12 users × 12 months | **₹115,200** |
| Setup (migration, channels, integrations, training) | One-time | — | **₹60,000** |

**Year 1 total investment: ₹463,200**

**Year 2+ recurring only: ₹403,200/year** (no setup)

### Assumptions for benefits

You interview the team and estimate:

| Source | Time saved per person per week | Tool |
|--------|-------------------------------|------|
| Faster drafting, summaries, research | 1.0 hour | Claude |
| Less email, faster answers, searchable history | 1.5 hours | Slack |
| **Total** | **2.5 hours/week** | |

**Team math:**

- 2.5 hours × 12 people × 52 weeks = **1,560 hours/year**
- Fully loaded hourly cost: **₹400/hour** (₹60,000/month ÷ 150 hours, loaded)
- **Annual value of time saved: 1,560 × ₹400 = ₹624,000**

**Hard benefit total (Year 1): ₹624,000**

You also note soft benefits separately: fewer "where is that file?" messages, cleaner client communication, onboarding new hires into channels instead of forwarding email chains. You do not add these to the number.

### Year 1 ROI calculation

```
Net Benefit = ₹624,000 − ₹463,200 = ₹160,800

ROI = (₹160,800 ÷ ₹463,200) × 100 = 34.7%
```

**Year 1 ROI: approximately 35%**

### Payback period

Monthly recurring cost = (₹288,000 + ₹115,200) ÷ 12 = **₹33,600/month**

Monthly benefit = ₹624,000 ÷ 12 = **₹52,000/month**

Monthly net benefit (after subscriptions) = ₹52,000 − ₹33,600 = **₹18,400/month**

Payback including setup:

```
Payback = ₹463,200 ÷ ₹18,400 ≈ 25.2 months
```

That looks weak—but it double-counts setup across the full year. A cleaner approach: **cumulative cash flow by month.**

| Month | Cash out | Cash in (benefit) | Net month | Cumulative |
|-------|----------|-------------------|-----------|------------|
| 1 | ₹93,600 (setup ₹60,000 + subs ₹33,600) | ₹52,000 | −₹41,600 | −₹41,600 |
| 2 | ₹33,600 | ₹52,000 | +₹18,400 | −₹23,200 |
| 3 | ₹33,600 | ₹52,000 | +₹18,400 | −₹4,800 |
| 4 | ₹33,600 | ₹52,000 | +₹18,400 | +₹13,600 |

**Payback: about 4 months** from go-live (cumulative turns positive in month 4).

The one-time setup makes Month 1 ugly. Payback under five months is what you put in front of Ma'am.

### Year 2 (no setup cost)

```
Net Benefit = ₹624,000 − ₹403,200 = ₹220,800

ROI = (₹220,800 ÷ ₹403,200) × 100 = 54.8%
```

### What you say to Ma'am

> "Year 1 all-in cost is ₹4.63 lakh—subscriptions plus ₹60,000 setup. We estimate 2.5 hours saved per person per week at ₹400/hour loaded cost, worth ₹6.24 lakh annually. Net benefit Year 1 is ₹1.6 lakh; ROI about 35%. We break even in roughly four months after launch. Year 2 ROI rises to ~55% because setup does not repeat. If we only capture half the time savings, we still break even in about eight months."

That is a purchase justification. It has numbers, assumptions, and a downside case.

### Conservative stress test (50% time savings)

- Benefit: ₹312,000/year
- Year 1 net: ₹312,000 − ₹463,200 = **−₹151,200** (negative)
- Monthly net after subs: ₹26,000 − ₹33,600 = **−₹7,600/month**

At half the estimated productivity gain, the purchase **does not pay back in Year 1**. You would either negotiate fewer seats, phase rollout (start with six users), or wait until the team is larger so the fixed setup cost spreads over more benefit.

That is also a valid outcome of the exercise.

## 6. YOUR TURN

### Problem

Your eight-person startup is evaluating a project management tool.

| Item | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Subscription | ₹1,500/user/month |
| Setup and training | ₹25,000 (one-time) |
| Expected time saved | 2 hours/person/week |
| Fully loaded hourly cost | ₹350/hour |

Calculate:

1. Year 1 total investment
2. Annual value of time saved
3. Year 1 net benefit
4. Year 1 ROI (%)
5. Payback month (using monthly cumulative cash flow, setup in month 1)

### Answer key

**1. Year 1 total investment**

- Subscriptions: ₹1,500 × 8 × 12 = **₹144,000**
- Setup: **₹25,000**
- **Total: ₹169,000**

**2. Annual value of time saved**

- Hours: 2 × 8 × 52 = 416 hours
- Value: 416 × ₹350 = **₹145,600**

**3. Year 1 net benefit**

- ₹145,600 − ₹169,000 = **−₹23,400** (negative in Year 1)

**4. Year 1 ROI**

- ROI = (−₹23,400 ÷ ₹169,000) × 100 = **−13.8%**

**5. Payback month**

- Monthly subscription: ₹144,000 ÷ 12 = ₹12,000
- Monthly benefit: ₹145,600 ÷ 12 = ₹12,133
- Month 1 net: −₹25,000 − ₹12,000 + ₹12,133 = **−₹24,867**
- Month 2 onward net: ₹12,133 − ₹12,000 = **+₹133/month**

Cumulative after Month 2: −₹24,867 + ₹133 = −₹24,734. The monthly surplus is only ₹133—it would take ~187 more months to recover. **Payback does not occur within a reasonable horizon** at these assumptions.

**Lesson:** A tool can be "useful" and still fail ROI at current team size. Options: fewer seats, cheaper tier, or revisit when the team doubles.

## 7. PRACTICE QUESTIONS

### MCQ 1

A startup spends ₹200,000 on a tool in Year 1 (including ₹50,000 one-time setup) and measures ₹280,000 in hard benefits for the same year. What is the Year 1 ROI?

- A) 140%
- B) 40%
- C) 28%
- D) 80%

**Answer: B) 40%**

**Explanation:**

```
Net Benefit = ₹280,000 − ₹200,000 = ₹80,000

ROI = (₹80,000 ÷ ₹200,000) × 100 = 40%
```

Option A (140%) is a common mistake—dividing benefit by investment without subtracting investment first. ROI is return on net gain, not gross benefit ratio.

---

### MCQ 2

Which payback calculation is most appropriate when a purchase has a large one-time setup cost in the first month and recurring subscriptions thereafter?

- A) Divide annual benefit by monthly subscription cost
- B) Divide total Year 1 investment by monthly gross benefit (ignoring recurring costs)
- C) Build a monthly cumulative cash flow table including setup in month 1, then find when cumulative turns positive
- D) Use Year 2 ROI because setup does not repeat

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:**

Option C is correct because payback is a cash-timing question. A lump-sum setup in month 1 distorts simple formulas. Cumulative monthly cash flow shows when the investment is actually recovered.

- A ignores setup and nets only against subscription.
- B overstates payback speed by ignoring that subscriptions continue every month.
- D is useful for comparing steady-state returns but does not answer "when do we break even after launch?"

# CHAPTER 6: How Do I Spot Problems Before My Accountant Explains Them Away?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

You are three months from your next fundraise. Revenue is up 18% quarter on quarter. Your accountant sends a clean P&L and says margins are "stable." The board deck looks fine. Then your operations lead mentions that two key suppliers have tightened credit terms. Your collections team is chasing invoices that are 45 days past due. Payroll cleared last month only because you delayed a vendor payment.

You ask your accountant: "Are we okay?"

They reply with precision: "Net profit is positive. Depreciation is non-cash. Receivables timing is normal for your sector. Working capital will normalize."

Every word may be technically correct. None of it tells you whether you will run out of cash in sixty days.

This is the **credibility trap**: the gap between financial statements that are *accurate* and financial reality that is *actionable*. Accountants are trained to record, classify, and comply. Founders are paid to survive, allocate scarce cash, and make decisions under uncertainty. When you rely only on lagging reports and professional reassurance, you discover problems when options are already narrow—during a crunch, a down round, or an emergency bridge.

This chapter gives you a founder's early-warning toolkit: how to read a balance sheet, track working capital, separate profit from cash, run simple variance analysis, and follow the **funds flow** so you see trouble while you can still fix it.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Startups rarely die because they forgot to book revenue. They die because:

- **Cash runs out** while the P&L still shows a profit.
- **Working capital silently absorbs** every rupee of growth.
- **Costs drift** faster than anyone updates the plan.
- **Balance sheet stress** hides behind "one-time" adjustments and footnotes.

Spotting these patterns early changes your leverage. You can renegotiate terms before suppliers cut you off. You can fix pricing before discounting becomes a habit. You can cut burn with surgical precision instead of panic layoffs.

You do not need to become an accountant. You need to ask better questions *before* the monthly close becomes a narrative. The goal is not to catch your accountant in a mistake. The goal is to build **financial reflexes**—a short set of checks you run every month that surface divergence between story and substance.

Three outcomes when you do this well:

1. **Earlier intervention** — You act in weeks, not after a qualified audit opinion.
2. **Better capital decisions** — You know whether you need equity, debt, or operational fixes.
3. **Credibility with investors** — You explain variances yourself instead of being explained to.

---

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 The Credibility Trap

The credibility trap appears when **technical correctness replaces managerial clarity**. Statements prepared under accounting standards reflect economic events with accrual timing, estimates, and policy choices. That is not the same as **liquidity**, **runway**, or **unit economics under stress**.

Common trap phrases and what they often mask:

| Phrase | What it may obscure |
|--------|---------------------|
| "It's a timing difference" | Persistent working capital drain |
| "Non-cash charge" | Real cash went out elsewhere |
| "Normalized EBITDA" | Costs excluded may return next quarter |
| "Seasonal" | Structural collection or demand weakness |

Your defense is not skepticism for its own sake. It is **triangulation**: compare profit, cash, and balance sheet movement together.

### 3.2 Profit vs Cash

**Profit** (net income) measures performance over a period using accrual accounting. Revenue is recognized when earned; expenses when incurred—whether or not cash moved.

**Cash flow** measures actual rupees in and out. A startup can be profitable and illiquid, or loss-making but cash-rich (e.g., after a fundraise).

The bridge between them lives largely on the **balance sheet**:

- Revenue booked → **Accounts receivable** (cash not yet received)
- Expenses booked → **Accounts payable** or **accrued expenses** (cash not yet paid)
- Inventory purchased → **Inventory** (cash already out, profit not yet earned)

Hence the founder's version of a classic line: **profit is an opinion; cash is a fact**.

### 3.3 Working Capital and the Current Ratio

**Working capital** = Current Assets − Current Liabilities

It is the capital tied up in the short operating cycle: inventory, receivables, prepaid expenses, minus what you owe suppliers and others in the next twelve months.

**Current ratio** = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

Interpretation (manufacturing and trading contexts; SaaS differs):

- **Below 1.0** — Short-term obligations exceed liquid resources; stress unless cash is incoming and reliable.
- **1.0 to 1.5** — Tight but workable if collections are strong.
- **Above 1.5** — More buffer; very high ratios may mean idle cash or poor receivable/collection management.

For growth companies, watch not just the level but the **trend**. Rising revenue with falling current ratio often signals that growth is **self-financing** through receivables and inventory—a classic pre-crisis pattern.

**Quick ratio** (acid-test) = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities — stricter, useful when inventory is slow-moving.

### 3.4 Funds Flow (Sources and Uses)

**Funds-flow analysis** asks: where did money come from, and where did it go?

At a simplified level for founders:

**Sources of funds**

- Operating profit (before non-cash items)
- Increase in current liabilities (e.g., stretching payables)
- New equity or debt
- Sale of fixed assets

**Uses of funds**

- Operating losses
- Increase in current assets (receivables, inventory)
- Capex and asset purchases
- Debt repayment
- Dividends or buybacks

If uses consistently exceed internal sources, external funding or cost reduction is not optional—it is arithmetic.

A monthly **sources-and-uses table** (even one page) beats a thirty-page annual report for decision-making.

### 3.5 Variance Analysis

**Variance** = Actual − Budget (or Actual − Prior Period)

Split variances into:

- **Volume variance** — Did we sell/produce more or less than planned?
- **Price/rate variance** — Did we achieve planned price or cost per unit?
- **Mix variance** — Did the blend of products/channels change?
- **Fixed cost variance** — Did overhead drift?

For startups, the highest-signal report is often the **cost report with variance analysis**: budget vs actual by line item, with **favorable (F)** and **unfavorable (U)** flags—not to assign blame, but to see **where the plan broke**.

A 10% revenue beat means little if COGS ran 22% over plan because of scrap, freight, or vendor price increases nobody escalated.

### 3.6 Reading the Balance Sheet as a Founder

The balance sheet is a **snapshot of claims and resources** at a date—not a flow. Read it in layers:

**Layer 1 — Liquidity (top)**  
Cash, bank balances, receivables, inventory. Ask: How much is truly available within 30 days?

**Layer 2 — Operating cycle**  
Compare receivable days, inventory days, and payable days. Are you financing customers longer than suppliers finance you?

**Layer 3 — Solvency and structure**  
Debt vs equity, related-party balances, contingent liabilities. Growth funded by short-term debt is fragile.

**Layer 4 — Quality of assets**  
Old receivables, obsolete inventory, capitalized software that may need impairment—each can inflate apparent health.

Cross-check: **Balance sheet movement + P&L + cash flow statement** should tell one coherent story. If they do not, that is your signal to dig—not to defer.

---

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

Use a **monthly founder finance ritual** (60–90 minutes):

### Step 1: Cash truth first

Open the bank statement and cash flow summary. Note:

- Opening cash
- Operating cash in/out
- Closing cash
- Runway at current net burn

Ignore profit for the first ten minutes.

### Step 2: Working capital dashboard

Track four numbers month over month:

| Metric | Formula |
|--------|---------|
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities |
| Receivable days | (Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 30 |
| Inventory days | (Inventory ÷ COGS) × 30 |
| Payable days | (Payables ÷ COGS) × 30 |

Plot trends. One bad month is noise; three consecutive moves in the wrong direction is a pattern.

### Step 3: Profit vs cash reconciliation (simplified)

Take net profit and adjust:

```
Net profit
+ Depreciation and other non-cash charges
− Increase in receivables
− Increase in inventory
+ Increase in payables
≈ Approximate operating cash impact
```

Compare to actual operating cash flow. Large gaps mean something is missing—prepayments, GST timing, capex in wrong bucket, or related-party movements.

### Step 4: Variance analysis on costs

For each major cost line (people, cloud, logistics, marketing, contractors):

- Actual vs budget ₹ and %
- Classify: volume, rate, or one-time
- Owner assigned for next month

Escalate only **unfavorable trends**, not single blips.

### Step 5: Funds-flow one-pager

Summarize:

- Did working capital absorb or release cash?
- Did we raise or repay debt?
- Did capex spike?

End with: **Surplus or deficit this month, and cumulative for the quarter.**

### Step 6: Questions for your accountant (before they explain)

Bring specifics:

- "Receivables rose ₹X but revenue rose only ₹Y—who are the top five debtors and what is overdue?"
- "Current ratio moved from 1.4 to 1.1—was it payables, debt, or receivables?"
- "Which variances over 10% are structural vs one-off?"

You are not auditing them. You are **forcing linkage** between numbers and operations.

**NovaKitchen Pvt Ltd** sells smart kitchen appliances online and through two retail chains. You are the founder reviewing October results.

### Given data (₹ in lakhs)

| Item | Sep | Oct | Budget (Oct) |
|------|-----|-----|--------------|
| Revenue | 420 | 510 | 480 |
| COGS | 252 | 336 | 288 |
| Gross profit | 168 | 174 | 192 |
| Operating expenses | 130 | 148 | 135 |
| Net profit | 38 | 26 | 57 |
| Cash (bank) | 85 | 62 | — |
| Accounts receivable | 140 | 210 | — |
| Inventory | 95 | 118 | — |
| Accounts payable | 110 | 105 | — |
| Current liabilities (total) | 160 | 175 | — |
| Current assets (total) | 330 | 400 | — |

Depreciation (non-cash) in October: ₹8 lakh.

### Analysis

**1. Profit vs cash**

Net profit fell from ₹38L to ₹26L (−32%). Cash fell from ₹85L to ₹62L (−27%). So the P&L already looks weaker—but cash did not fall *only* because of profit.

Receivables increased by ₹70L (210 − 140). Inventory by ₹23L. Payables **fell** by ₹5L (you paid suppliers faster or ordered less on credit).

Approximate operating cash impact from working capital:

- Receivables up ₹70L → cash **out**
- Inventory up ₹23L → cash **out**
- Payables down ₹5L → cash **out**
- Subtotal working capital drain ≈ **₹98L**

Add back depreciation ₹8L (non-cash). Profit ₹26L plus depreciation ₹8L = ₹34L "paper" generation before working capital—nowhere near enough to offset a ₹98L working capital hit. That explains why cash dropped sharply despite "still profitable."

**2. Current ratio**

September: 330 ÷ 160 = **2.06**  
October: 400 ÷ 175 = **2.29**

Ratio improved—but misleadingly. Receivables and inventory (less liquid, quality risk) drove asset growth, not cash. **Superficially healthy ratio, deteriorating cash.**

**3. Variance analysis (October)**

| Line | Actual | Budget | Variance | Note |
|------|--------|--------|----------|------|
| Revenue | 510 | 480 | +30 F | Volume and one retail promotion |
| COGS | 336 | 288 | +48 U | Higher material cost + air freight for stockout |
| OpEx | 148 | 135 | +13 U | Overtime and contractor surge |
| Net profit | 26 | 57 | −31 U | Revenue beat did not cover cost overruns |

Revenue variance is **favorable**. COGS variance is **unfavorable** and larger. This is the classic **growth trap**: top line beats plan; unit economics worsen.

COGS rate: 336/510 = **65.9%** vs budget 288/480 = **60%**. You sold more but at worse margin—promotional mix and expedited freight.

**4. Funds flow (October, simplified)**

| Sources | ₹L | Uses | ₹L |
|---------|-----|------|-----|
| Net profit | 26 | Δ Receivables | 70 |
| Depreciation (add back) | 8 | Δ Inventory | 23 |
| | | Δ Payables (reduction) | 5 |
| | | Δ Cash (net decrease) | 23* |

*Cash change 85 → 62 = 23; rounding and other WC items may apply in full books.

**Diagnosis before the accountant softens it:** October was not a "good growth month." It was a month of **subsidized revenue** funded by working capital and margin compression. Without action, November collections must fund October's story—or cash worsens.

**5. Founder actions**

- Stop promotion until COGS rate back under 62%.
- Collections sprint on top five receivable accounts (₹210L outstanding).
- Renegotiate supplier terms to restore payables days.
- Weekly 13-week cash forecast; tie marketing spend to collected cash, not booked revenue.

You spotted the problem **from the balance sheet and variances**, not from net profit alone.

# CHAPTER 7: How Do I Use All of This When Running My Startup?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

You are the founder of **NovaCraft** — a 10-person startup in Bengaluru that designs and sells modular home-office furniture to remote workers and small companies. Revenue is growing. So is the chaos.

Over the past year you learned seven separate skills: which numbers to trust (Chapter 1), whether you are actually making money (Chapter 2), what things really cost (Chapter 3), when you stop losing money (Chapter 4), how to justify a purchase with ROI (Chapter 5), and how to spot trouble before your accountant smooths it over (Chapter 6).

Now it is April. Your year ahead has four real decisions — one per quarter — and each one pulls a different tool off the shelf:

| Quarter | Decision on your desk |
|---|---|
| **Q1 (Apr–Jun)** | Launch a budget line called **NovaLite** at ₹9,500 per desk? |
| **Q2 (Jul–Sep)** | Buy **Claude + Slack** for the team — ma'am wants ROI in writing |
| **Q3 (Oct–Dec)** | Profit is down 38%. Cash is worse. What broke? |
| **Q4 (Jan–Mar)** | A seed investor wants audited books. Outsource assembly or buy equipment? |

None of these is "an accounting question." They are **CEO questions** that accounting helps you answer. This chapter shows you how to run all seven skills as one operating rhythm — not seven separate homework topics.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Founders who learn finance in pieces often freeze when real decisions arrive. They know break-even exists. They know ROI is a percentage. They cannot connect either to a Tuesday morning when payroll is due, a client wants a discount, and an investor emails asking for statements.

The gap is not knowledge. It is **integration**.

Anil Lamba's core message across *Romancing the Balance Sheet* is that financial management is not clerical — it is the intellectual work of connecting actions to outcomes. As CEO you are not the scorekeeper. You are the player who picks which shot to take. But you cannot play blind.

**Without integration, three failures repeat:**

1. **Wrong tool, wrong decision** — Using last year's audited P&L to decide whether to accept a discount order (FA when you need MA).
2. **Right answer, wrong timing** — Approving a tool purchase with strong ROI while cash is trapped in receivables and inventory.
3. **Trusting the headline** — Celebrating profit while working capital drains the bank account.

The chapters in this kit were deliberately sequenced: trust → statements → cost → break-even → ROI → early warning → **this chapter**. You are not learning accounting. You are building a decision reflex.

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 The CEO Decision Map

Every financial question you face maps to one of two systems and one of three question types.

**System filter (Chapter 1):**

| Internal (you, your team) | Future, flexible | **Managerial Accounting** — cost analysis, break-even, ROI, variance |

                    │         WHAT ARE YOU ASKING?        │

| Decision type | Primary chapters | Key outputs |
|---|---|---|
| Loan, investment, compliance | Ch 1, 2 | Audited FA statements |

| Growth vs survival | Ch 3, 4, 6 | Break-even + working capital together |

No single report answers every question. The CEO's job is to know **which report answers which question** — then ask for it before deciding.

### 3.2 The Monthly CEO Rhythm

Accounting runs on periods. So should you. A 60-minute monthly rhythm beats a quarterly panic.

**Week 1 — Cash truth (15 min)**  
Open the bank statement. Not the P&L. Ask: *What is our cash runway at current burn?* If cash and profit diverge, flag it immediately (Chapter 2).

**Week 2 — Statement scan (20 min)**  
Review P&L and Balance Sheet your CA sends. Three numbers only:
- Revenue trend (top line)
- Net profit (bottom line)
- Cash from operations on Cash Flow Statement

Ask: *Do these three tell the same story?* If profit is up and operating cash is down, go to Chapter 6 before making new commitments.

**Week 3 — MA check (15 min)**  
One internal number: budget vs actual on your biggest cost line, or contribution per unit on your top product. This is variance analysis in miniature (Chapter 6). Catches problems while they are small.

**Week 4 — Decision queue (10 min)**  
List every pending decision. Tag each FA or MA using the filter from Chapter 1. Anything MA-tagged gets a one-page memo: cost, benefit, break-even or ROI, cash impact.

**Quarterly (half day):** Walk through all four decision types on the map. Update forecasts. This is when NovaCraft's quarterly decisions get made — not ad hoc in Slack threads.

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

### The Integrated Decision Protocol

For any decision, run five steps in order. Skipping a step is how smart founders make dumb bets.

**Step 1 — Classify**  
FA or MA? External or internal? Past or future?

**Step 2 — Pull the right numbers**  
| If deciding… | Pull… |
|---|---|
| Launch / price / discount | Variable cost, contribution, break-even |
| Buy / hire / invest | Total cost, expected benefit, payback, ROI |
| Explain performance | P&L + Cash Flow + variance |
| Raise money / borrow | Audited FA package |

**Step 3 — Run the math**  
Use the formulas from Chapters 3–5. Write it down. One page. Numbers, not vibes.

**Step 4 — Cash check**  
Even a great ROI fails if you cannot fund the upfront cost. Check bank balance, receivables, inventory, and payables (Chapter 6). **Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact.**

**Step 5 — Decide and log**  
Record the decision, the numbers used, and the review date. In three months you will variance-analyze your own forecast.

### The Quarterly Review Template

At each quarter-end, answer these four questions:

1. **Performance:** Did revenue and profit move as expected? (Ch 2)
2. **Unit economics:** Is contribution per unit still healthy? (Ch 3–4)
3. **Cash position:** Where is money trapped? (Ch 6)
4. **Pipeline:** What decisions are coming next quarter? Tag each FA/MA. (Ch 1)

### Q1 Decision: Launch NovaLite?

**Situation:** Product team proposes a simpler desk at ₹9,500 for students and gig workers. Additional fixed costs for the line: ₹1,80,000/month (part-time designer + targeted ads). Variable cost per NovaLite unit: ₹5,800.

**Tools used:** Chapters 3–4 (contribution, break-even)

```
Contribution per unit = ₹9,500 − ₹5,800 = ₹3,700

```

| Setup + training | One-time | ₹60,000 |
| **Year 1 total cost** | | **₹93,600** |

**Benefit estimate:** Each team member saves 2 hours/week on drafting, searching files, and status updates. Loaded cost of time: ₹500/hour.

```
Hours saved/year = 2 hrs × 12 people × 48 weeks = 1,152 hours
Value of time saved = 1,152 × ₹500 = ₹5,76,000/year
```

**ROI (Year 1):**

```
Net benefit = ₹5,76,000 − ₹93,600 = ₹4,82,400

ROI = (Net benefit ÷ Total cost) × 100
    = (₹4,82,400 ÷ ₹93,600) × 100
    = 515%
```

**Payback:**

```
Monthly benefit = ₹5,76,000 ÷ 12 = ₹48,000
Payback = ₹93,600 ÷ ₹48,000 = 1.95 months ≈ 2 months
```

**Cash check:** ₹93,600 Year 1 cost is less than 5% of bank balance. Upfront ₹60,000 setup is affordable.

**Decision: APPROVE.** Strong MA case. This is not an FA decision — no auditor needs to sign off. Revisit benefit assumptions in Q4.

### Q3 Decision: Profit down, cash worse — what broke?

**Situation:** October board update. P&L shows profit of ₹4,20,000 for the quarter — down from ₹6,80,000 last quarter. Bank balance dropped ₹8,00,000. Your CA says "seasonal dip." You are not convinced.

**Tools used:** Chapters 2, 6 (profit vs cash, variance, working capital)

**Variance analysis (materials — biggest cost line):**

| | Budget | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material cost | ₹9,00,000 | ₹10,62,000 | **+18% unfavourable** |

Plywood prices rose. Nobody updated pricing. Contribution per core NovaDesk unit fell from ₹5,900 to ₹4,700.

**Funds-flow / balance sheet reading:**

| Item | Last quarter | This quarter | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | ₹18,00,000 | ₹10,00,000 | −₹8,00,000 |
| Receivables | ₹6,00,000 | ₹18,00,000 | +₹12,00,000 |
| Inventory | ₹4,00,000 | ₹9,00,000 | +₹5,00,000 |
| Payables | ₹5,00,000 | ₹7,00,000 | +₹2,00,000 |

**Current ratio:**

```
Current Assets = ₹10,00,000 + ₹18,00,000 + ₹9,00,000 = ₹37,00,000
Current Liabilities = ₹7,00,000
Current Ratio = ₹37,00,000 ÷ ₹7,00,000 = 5.3
```

Ratio looks healthy. **This is the credibility trap** (Chapter 6): receivables and inventory inflate current assets, masking a cash crisis. Profit says ₹4.2L earned. Cash says ₹8L left — because ₹12L is stuck in a corporate client's 60-day payment terms and ₹5L sits in unsold NovaLite stock.

**Actions:**
1. Renegotiate client terms to 30 days or require 50% advance.
2. Slow NovaLite production to match sell-through.
3. Pass 8% of material cost increase into NovaDesk pricing next month.
4. Weekly cash forecast until receivables normalise.

**Decision: DO NOT launch any new spend.** Fix working capital first. ROI on new tools means nothing if payroll bounces.

### Q4 Decision: Seed round + outsource vs own assembly?

**Situation:** A seed investor wants audited FY statements and a 12-month plan. You must also decide: outsource assembly (₹2,200/desk variable) or buy equipment (₹25,00,000 upfront, in-house variable drops to ₹1,750/desk). Projected volume: 800 desks/year.

**Tools used:** Chapters 1, 2, 3 (FA for investor, make vs buy, cash runway)

**FA package for investor (summary):**

| Statement | Key figure |
|---|---|
| P&L — Revenue | ₹1,20,00,000 |
| P&L — Net profit | ₹18,00,000 (15% margin) |
| Cash Flow — Operating activities | **(₹4,00,000)** negative |
| Balance Sheet — Net working capital | Tight after Q3; improving with collection fixes |

Investor sees profitable company with a cash conversion problem — honest FA disclosure builds trust (Chapter 1). You pair it with an MA plan showing Q3 fixes.

**Make vs buy at 800 units/year:**

| | Outsource | In-house (new equipment) |
|---|---|---|
| Variable cost/unit | ₹2,200 | ₹1,750 |
| Annual variable | ₹17,60,000 | ₹14,00,000 |
| Upfront capex | ₹0 | ₹25,00,000 |
| **Year 1 total cash** | **₹17,60,000** | **₹39,00,000** |

In-house saves ₹3,60,000/year in variable cost but ties up ₹25L that funds 14 months of fixed costs at current burn.

**Cash check:** Post-Q3 recovery, bank balance is ₹14,00,000. Equipment purchase would leave ₹(11,00,000) before the round closes.

**Decision: Outsource for 12 months.** Revisit in-house when (a) volume exceeds 1,500 units/year, (b) seed round closes, (c) operating cash flow is positive for two consecutive quarters. Send investor audited statements (FA) plus the outsource plan with break-even at higher volume (MA).

## 6. YOUR TURN

**Company:** FreshBite — meal-prep delivery, 8 employees, Pune.

**Quarterly decisions:**

1. **Q1:** New ₹299 lunch box. Variable cost ₹185. Extra fixed ₹95,000/month. Projected 420 boxes/month. Launch?
2. **Q2:** Delivery bikes on lease — ₹8,000/bike/month × 4 bikes vs hiring riders on commission (₹40/delivery, 600 deliveries/month). Which is cheaper on variable basis?
3. **Q3:** Profit ₹1,80,000 but cash down ₹3,50,000. Receivables up ₹2,80,000, raw food inventory up ₹1,20,000. Name the problem and one action.
4. **Q4:** Cloud kitchen investor wants last year's P&L and Cash Flow. Your ops lead sends a budget forecast instead. Correct that mistake in one sentence.

**Answer key:**

1. Contribution = ₹299 − ₹185 = ₹114. Break-even = ₹95,000 ÷ ₹114 = 833 boxes. At 420 boxes, line loses money. **Do not launch** unless volume forecast doubles or fixed cost drops.
2. Lease = ₹32,000/month fixed. Commission = 600 × ₹40 = ₹24,000 variable. Commission is cheaper at 600 deliveries. Revisit at 800+ deliveries when lease may win.
3. **Working capital trap** — profit on paper, cash stuck in receivables and perishable inventory. Action: collect outstanding payments within 7 days; reduce raw stock to 3-day cover.
4. **Investor needs FA (audited historical P&L and Cash Flow), not an MA budget forecast.** Send the right document.

**Question B**  
NovaCraft shows ₹4,20,000 quarterly profit but cash fell ₹8,00,000. Receivables rose ₹12,00,000 and inventory rose ₹5,00,000. The current ratio is 5.3. What is the BEST next step?

A) Raise prices immediately because the current ratio is weak  
B) Invest in new equipment to lower unit costs  
C) Accelerate collections and reduce excess inventory before approving new spending  
D) Switch from MA to FA reporting  

**Answer: C** — High current ratio masks illiquid assets. Cash is trapped in receivables and stock. Fix working capital first (Chapter 6).

**Question C**  
NovaCraft evaluates Claude + Slack: Year 1 cost ₹93,600, expected benefit ₹5,76,000. A colleague says "reject it because the Cash Flow Statement shows negative operating cash." What is the BEST response?

A) Negative operating cash means all purchases must be rejected  
B) ROI analysis (MA) supports the purchase if cash can fund the upfront cost; fix Q3 collection issues separately  
C) ROI is irrelevant; only the audited P&L matters  
D) Wait until operating cash is positive for three years  

**Answer: B** — Tool ROI is an MA forward decision. Past operating cash weakness (FA) is a separate problem. Both checks matter; one does not automatically veto the other if cash can fund the purchase and collections are being fixed.

## Formula Reference Sheet

### Financial Statements (Chapter 2)

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Profit / Loss | Profit = Income − Expenses |
| COGS | Opening Inventory + Purchases − Closing Inventory |
| Balance Sheet equation | Assets = Liabilities + Equity |
| Current Ratio | Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities |
| Net Working Capital | Current Assets − Current Liabilities |

### Cost & Contribution (Chapter 3)

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Contribution per unit | Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit |
| Total contribution | Units sold × Contribution per unit |
| Profit | Contribution − Fixed Costs |
| PV Ratio | Contribution ÷ Sales |

### Break-Even (Chapter 4)

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Break-even units | Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit |
| Break-even sales (₹) | Break-even units × Selling Price |
| Profit at volume | (Units × Contribution per unit) − Fixed Costs |

### ROI & Payback (Chapter 5)

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total cost of purchase | One-time costs + Recurring costs |
| Net benefit | Total expected benefit − Total cost |
| ROI (%) | (Net benefit ÷ Total cost) × 100 |
| Payback period | Total cost ÷ Monthly benefit |

### Early Warning (Chapter 6)

| Concept | Formula / Signal |
|---|---|
| Budget variance | Actual − Budget (favourable if cost is lower) |
| Profit vs cash gap | Compare P&L profit to operating cash flow |
| Funds-flow logic | Profit + non-cash items ± working capital changes ≈ cash movement |
| Credibility trap | Healthy ratios (e.g. current ratio) while cash is low due to receivables/inventory |

### Decision Classification (Chapter 1)

| Signal | Use |
|---|---|
| External audience + past period | Financial Accounting |
| Internal decision + future estimate | Managerial Accounting |

## What to Study Next

You now have a CEO-level finance kit — not a CA qualification. The natural next steps depend on where NovaCraft (or your real company) is on the growth curve.

**If you are pre-revenue or early revenue:**  
Re-read Chapters 3–4 monthly. Break-even and contribution are your survival tools. Keep fixed costs variable as long as possible. Track cash weekly, not quarterly.

**If you are raising your first institutional round:**  
Re-read Chapters 1–2 and 6. Investors read FA; you explain the MA story behind the numbers. Practice walking through your last three statements out loud in under ten minutes. Know why operating cash differed from profit before they ask.

**If you are scaling past 20 people:**  
Deepen variance analysis and departmental budgeting. Add segment reporting — profit per product line, per city, per customer type. Lamba's full book covers funds-flow statements, leverage, and inventory accounting in more depth than this kit.

**Recommended reading (in order):**  
1. *Romancing the Balance Sheet* — Anil Lamba (full book; this kit drew from Chapters 1–3 and 11)  
2. *Financial Intelligence for Entrepreneurs* — Karen Berman & Joe Knight (management-friendly ratios)  
3. *The Lean CFO* — Kurtis Nanette (startup-specific finance operations)

**Practice habit:** Pick one real decision each month. Run the five-step protocol from Chapter 7 on paper. File it. Review after 90 days. That loop — decide, measure, variance-analyze — is how managerial accounting becomes instinct.

You are not a non-finance person anymore. You are a CEO who knows which numbers to trust, when to trust them, and what to do when they disagree.
