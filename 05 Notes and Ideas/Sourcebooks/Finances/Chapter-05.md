# CHAPTER 5: How Do I Justify a Purchase and Figure Out ROI?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

Your startup has twelve people. Everyone is on WhatsApp for work, Google Docs for drafts, and email for anything that falls between. It works, but it does not scale. Messages get lost. Someone asks the same question three times in a week. A founder spends forty-five minutes rewriting a client email that could have taken ten.

You propose two tools: **Claude** for drafting, summarising, and research, and **Slack** for team communication. The combined cost is not trivial—subscriptions for twelve people, plus a one-time setup fee to migrate channels, train the team, and wire up integrations.

Your co-founder—let's call her Ma'am, because that is how half the startup ecosystem refers to the person who actually reads the numbers—asks a fair question:

> "Why are we spending this much? What is the ROI?"

She is not being difficult. She is doing her job. Every rupee in a twelve-person company is a rupee that could pay salary, extend runway, or fund a customer pilot. "Everyone uses Slack" is not a financial argument. "This saves us X hours per week, which is worth Y rupees, and we break even in Z months" is.

This chapter teaches you how to build that argument—and how to spot when a purchase does not deserve one.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Startups do not fail because founders bought the wrong SaaS tool. They fail because they ran out of money, or because they spent money on things that did not move the business forward.

Purchase justification is not bureaucracy. It is a habit of connecting spending to outcomes. When you justify a purchase properly, you get three things:

1. **Clarity for yourself.** You know whether you are buying productivity, revenue, or peace of mind—and whether the price matches.
2. **Credibility with investors and co-founders.** Finance people respect numbers, even rough ones. "I think it'll help" earns a nod. "Payback in nine months on loaded labour cost" earns a budget line.
3. **A decision framework for later.** When the next tool pitches you—CRM, analytics, AI copilot—you already know how to run the same math instead of debating vibes.

The goal is not to prove every purchase is worth it. Sometimes the honest answer is "no, not yet." That is also a good outcome.

## 3. THE THEORY

### Financial modeling for CEOs (the minimum you need)

You do not need an MBA spreadsheet. You need a simple model with four boxes:

| Box | Question it answers |
|-----|---------------------|
| **Costs** | What do we pay, when, and for how long? |
| **Benefits** | What money comes in, or what cost goes away? |
| **Timeline** | Over what period are we measuring? |
| **Assumptions** | What has to be true for this to work? |

Write the assumptions down. They are the part people argue about—and the part you must defend.

### ROI (Return on Investment)

ROI tells you how much you gained relative to what you spent.

```
ROI (%) = (Net Benefit ÷ Total Investment) × 100
```

Where:

- **Total Investment** = all costs (subscriptions, setup, training, migration time if you choose to count it)
- **Net Benefit** = Total measurable benefits minus Total Investment

**Example:** You invest ₹100,000 and generate ₹150,000 in measurable benefit. Net benefit = ₹50,000. ROI = 50%.

ROI is useful for comparing options. It is less useful when benefits are hard to measure (brand, morale, risk reduction). Do not fake precision on soft benefits—label them separately.

### Payback period

Payback answers a simpler question: **How long until this purchase has paid for itself?**

```
Payback (months) = Total Investment ÷ Monthly Net Benefit
```

Where **Monthly Net Benefit** = monthly measurable benefits minus monthly recurring costs.

Payback is what Ma'am often cares about more than ROI. A 200% ROI that takes four years to realise is worse for a startup than a 40% ROI that pays back in six months—because runway is measured in months, not ratios.

### Jargon, translated

| Term | Plain English |
|------|---------------|
| **Capex** | One-time spend (setup, hardware, implementation) |
| **Opex** | Ongoing spend (monthly subscriptions) |
| **Fully loaded cost** | Salary plus taxes, benefits, office, tools—what an employee actually costs per hour |
| **Hard benefit** | Rupees you can point to (revenue, cancelled subscription, fewer contractors) |
| **Soft benefit** | Real but fuzzy (faster decisions, fewer mistakes, happier team) |
| **Run rate** | What a cost or benefit looks like if sustained for a full year |
| **Sensitivity** | What happens if your assumptions are wrong—e.g. half the time savings |

When someone says "we need ROI," they usually mean: **show me payback on hard benefits, and tell me what assumptions you're betting on.**

---

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

Here is a repeatable five-step process. Use it for any tool, hire, or vendor.

### Step 1: List all costs (don't forget the hidden ones)

- Per-user subscription × users × months
- One-time setup (vendor fees, your engineer's time, migration)
- Training time (optional but honest—two hours × twelve people is twenty-four hours)
- Cancellation costs of old tools (if any lock-in)

Separate **Year 1** (includes one-time costs) from **Year 2+** (recurring only).

### Step 2: List measurable benefits

Ask: **What changes in rupees if this works?**

Common categories for productivity tools:

- **Time saved** → convert hours to rupees using fully loaded hourly cost
- **Revenue enabled** → faster sales cycle, more proposals sent, better conversion
- **Cost avoided** → cancelled tool, fewer support tickets, less contractor spend
- **Error reduction** → fewer refunds, fewer compliance fixes

If you cannot quantify it, put it in a "soft benefits" list. Do not smuggle it into the ROI number.

### Step 3: Convert time to money

This is where most startup ROI models live or die.

```
Hourly loaded cost = Monthly fully loaded salary ÷ Working hours per month
```

A common shortcut for Indian startups: if average fully loaded cost is ₹60,000/month and productive hours are ~150/month, hourly cost ≈ **₹400/hour**. Adjust for your team.

### Step 4: Calculate ROI and payback

Use Year 1 numbers for a conservative picture (because setup hits once). Show Year 2 ROI separately if recurring benefits continue but setup does not repeat.

### Step 5: Stress-test assumptions

Run three scenarios:

| Scenario | Time saved assumption |
|----------|----------------------|
| Conservative | 50% of your estimate |
| Base | Your honest estimate |
| Optimistic | 125% of your estimate |

If ROI is only positive in the optimistic case, the purchase is a bet—not a justified investment.

## 5. WORKED EXAMPLE: Claude + Slack for a 12-Person Startup

### The proposal

| Item | Rate | Calculation | Annual cost |
|------|------|-------------|-------------|
| Claude (per user) | ₹2,000/month | ₹2,000 × 12 users × 12 months | **₹288,000** |
| Slack (per user) | ₹800/month | ₹800 × 12 users × 12 months | **₹115,200** |
| Setup (migration, channels, integrations, training) | One-time | — | **₹60,000** |

**Year 1 total investment: ₹463,200**

**Year 2+ recurring only: ₹403,200/year** (no setup)

### Assumptions for benefits

You interview the team and estimate:

| Source | Time saved per person per week | Tool |
|--------|-------------------------------|------|
| Faster drafting, summaries, research | 1.0 hour | Claude |
| Less email, faster answers, searchable history | 1.5 hours | Slack |
| **Total** | **2.5 hours/week** | |

**Team math:**

- 2.5 hours × 12 people × 52 weeks = **1,560 hours/year**
- Fully loaded hourly cost: **₹400/hour** (₹60,000/month ÷ 150 hours, loaded)
- **Annual value of time saved: 1,560 × ₹400 = ₹624,000**

**Hard benefit total (Year 1): ₹624,000**

You also note soft benefits separately: fewer "where is that file?" messages, cleaner client communication, onboarding new hires into channels instead of forwarding email chains. You do not add these to the number.

### Year 1 ROI calculation

```
Net Benefit = ₹624,000 − ₹463,200 = ₹160,800

ROI = (₹160,800 ÷ ₹463,200) × 100 = 34.7%
```

**Year 1 ROI: approximately 35%**

### Payback period

Monthly recurring cost = (₹288,000 + ₹115,200) ÷ 12 = **₹33,600/month**

Monthly benefit = ₹624,000 ÷ 12 = **₹52,000/month**

Monthly net benefit (after subscriptions) = ₹52,000 − ₹33,600 = **₹18,400/month**

Payback including setup:

```
Payback = ₹463,200 ÷ ₹18,400 ≈ 25.2 months
```

That looks weak—but it double-counts setup across the full year. A cleaner approach: **cumulative cash flow by month.**

| Month | Cash out | Cash in (benefit) | Net month | Cumulative |
|-------|----------|-------------------|-----------|------------|
| 1 | ₹93,600 (setup ₹60,000 + subs ₹33,600) | ₹52,000 | −₹41,600 | −₹41,600 |
| 2 | ₹33,600 | ₹52,000 | +₹18,400 | −₹23,200 |
| 3 | ₹33,600 | ₹52,000 | +₹18,400 | −₹4,800 |
| 4 | ₹33,600 | ₹52,000 | +₹18,400 | +₹13,600 |

**Payback: about 4 months** from go-live (cumulative turns positive in month 4).

The one-time setup makes Month 1 ugly. Payback under five months is what you put in front of Ma'am.

### Year 2 (no setup cost)

```
Net Benefit = ₹624,000 − ₹403,200 = ₹220,800

ROI = (₹220,800 ÷ ₹403,200) × 100 = 54.8%
```

### What you say to Ma'am

> "Year 1 all-in cost is ₹4.63 lakh—subscriptions plus ₹60,000 setup. We estimate 2.5 hours saved per person per week at ₹400/hour loaded cost, worth ₹6.24 lakh annually. Net benefit Year 1 is ₹1.6 lakh; ROI about 35%. We break even in roughly four months after launch. Year 2 ROI rises to ~55% because setup does not repeat. If we only capture half the time savings, we still break even in about eight months."

That is a purchase justification. It has numbers, assumptions, and a downside case.

### Conservative stress test (50% time savings)

- Benefit: ₹312,000/year
- Year 1 net: ₹312,000 − ₹463,200 = **−₹151,200** (negative)
- Monthly net after subs: ₹26,000 − ₹33,600 = **−₹7,600/month**

At half the estimated productivity gain, the purchase **does not pay back in Year 1**. You would either negotiate fewer seats, phase rollout (start with six users), or wait until the team is larger so the fixed setup cost spreads over more benefit.

That is also a valid outcome of the exercise.

## 6. YOUR TURN

### Problem

Your eight-person startup is evaluating a project management tool.

| Item | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Subscription | ₹1,500/user/month |
| Setup and training | ₹25,000 (one-time) |
| Expected time saved | 2 hours/person/week |
| Fully loaded hourly cost | ₹350/hour |

Calculate:

1. Year 1 total investment
2. Annual value of time saved
3. Year 1 net benefit
4. Year 1 ROI (%)
5. Payback month (using monthly cumulative cash flow, setup in month 1)

### Answer key

**1. Year 1 total investment**

- Subscriptions: ₹1,500 × 8 × 12 = **₹144,000**
- Setup: **₹25,000**
- **Total: ₹169,000**

**2. Annual value of time saved**

- Hours: 2 × 8 × 52 = 416 hours
- Value: 416 × ₹350 = **₹145,600**

**3. Year 1 net benefit**

- ₹145,600 − ₹169,000 = **−₹23,400** (negative in Year 1)

**4. Year 1 ROI**

- ROI = (−₹23,400 ÷ ₹169,000) × 100 = **−13.8%**

**5. Payback month**

- Monthly subscription: ₹144,000 ÷ 12 = ₹12,000
- Monthly benefit: ₹145,600 ÷ 12 = ₹12,133
- Month 1 net: −₹25,000 − ₹12,000 + ₹12,133 = **−₹24,867**
- Month 2 onward net: ₹12,133 − ₹12,000 = **+₹133/month**

Cumulative after Month 2: −₹24,867 + ₹133 = −₹24,734. The monthly surplus is only ₹133—it would take ~187 more months to recover. **Payback does not occur within a reasonable horizon** at these assumptions.

**Lesson:** A tool can be "useful" and still fail ROI at current team size. Options: fewer seats, cheaper tier, or revisit when the team doubles.

## 7. PRACTICE QUESTIONS

### MCQ 1

A startup spends ₹200,000 on a tool in Year 1 (including ₹50,000 one-time setup) and measures ₹280,000 in hard benefits for the same year. What is the Year 1 ROI?

- A) 140%
- B) 40%
- C) 28%
- D) 80%

**Answer: B) 40%**

**Explanation:**

```
Net Benefit = ₹280,000 − ₹200,000 = ₹80,000

ROI = (₹80,000 ÷ ₹200,000) × 100 = 40%
```

Option A (140%) is a common mistake—dividing benefit by investment without subtracting investment first. ROI is return on net gain, not gross benefit ratio.

---

### MCQ 2

Which payback calculation is most appropriate when a purchase has a large one-time setup cost in the first month and recurring subscriptions thereafter?

- A) Divide annual benefit by monthly subscription cost
- B) Divide total Year 1 investment by monthly gross benefit (ignoring recurring costs)
- C) Build a monthly cumulative cash flow table including setup in month 1, then find when cumulative turns positive
- D) Use Year 2 ROI because setup does not repeat

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:**

Option C is correct because payback is a cash-timing question. A lump-sum setup in month 1 distorts simple formulas. Cumulative monthly cash flow shows when the investment is actually recovered.

- A ignores setup and nets only against subscription.
- B overstates payback speed by ignoring that subscriptions continue every month.
- D is useful for comparing steady-state returns but does not answer "when do we break even after launch?"
