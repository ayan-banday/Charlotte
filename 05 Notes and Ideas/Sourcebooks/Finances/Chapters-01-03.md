# CHAPTER 1: How Do I Know Which Numbers to Trust for Decisions?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

You run a 12-person startup called **BuildRight** — you sell project-management software to small construction firms.

Three requests land on your desk the same morning:

1. **Your accountant** sends last year's audited Profit & Loss Account and Balance Sheet.
2. **Your ops lead** asks for budget approval to try a new cloud server setup next quarter.
3. **Your manager (ma'am)** asks you to figure out ROI on buying Claude and Slack for the team.

Three different number requests. Three different types of thinking. If you treat them all the same — or hand everything to the accountant — you'll either move too slowly on internal decisions or look unprepared when a bank or investor asks questions.

Your first job as CEO is not to *prepare* these numbers. It is to know **which kind of accounting answers which question**.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Anil Lamba opens *Romancing the Balance Sheet* with a blunt idea: when someone says *"I'm not a finance person,"* they are really saying *"I don't care what the financial outcome of my actions is."* That is dangerous for a CEO.

Here is the split most founders miss:

| What people think | What is actually true |
|---|---|
| Finance = what accountants do in the back office | **Accounting** records the past; **finance** (financial management) means taking decisions with money consequences |
| Only the finance team needs to understand numbers | Every role — sales, ops, product — makes decisions that hit profit and cash |
| One set of numbers should answer every question | **Two systems** exist because **two audiences** exist: outsiders who need standards, and you who need speed and forecasts |

**Accounting** (in the narrow sense) is clerical: record what happened.  
**Financial management** is intellectual: decide what to do next based on financial impact.

As Lamba puts it: the accountant is the **scorekeeper** in cricket — they record the score. **You and your team are the players.** The scorekeeper does not win the match. Operational decisions win it.

You need both **Financial Accounting (FA)** and **Managerial Accounting (MA)**. Neither replaces the other.

---

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 Why are there two types of accounting?

Businesses face two fundamentally different needs:

**Need 1 — Accountability to the outside world**  
Investors, banks, tax authorities, and regulators ask: *"What did this company do, in a form we can compare and verify?"*  
That requires **rules everyone follows the same way**. That is **Financial Accounting**.

**Need 2 — Running the business day to day**  
You ask: *"Should we launch? What price? Buy this tool? Kill this product line? Can we afford next month's payroll?"*  
That requires **flexible analysis tailored to your decision**. That is **Managerial Accounting**.

Same company. Two lenses. Different questions.

---

### 3.2 Financial Accounting (FA) — the "books"

**What it is:** The standardized recording and reporting of what **already happened**, for **external** users.

**Why it is called "financial accounting":**  
- **Financial** — from Old French *finer*, "to pay, settle." It deals with the money outcomes of the business.  
- **Accounting** — from Latin *computare*, "to count." It is the formal counting and reporting system.

**Orientation:** Past. Last quarter. Last year.

**Audience:** External — banks, investors, regulators, shareholders, tax department.

**Rules:** **GAAP** (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) in the US, **IFRS** (International Financial Reporting Standards) in much of the world including India. These exist so Company A and Company B can be compared fairly.

**Why rules matter:** If every company invented its own format, a bank could not compare your startup to another. Standardization creates **comparability** and **reliability**.

**Timing:** Fixed periods — quarterly, annually — whether you feel like it or not.

**Typical outputs:**
- **Income Statement** (Profit & Loss Account)
- **Balance Sheet**
- **Cash Flow Statement**
- **Statement of Shareholders' Equity**

**What FA is NOT:** A forecast. A budget. A "what if we buy Slack" analysis.

---

### 3.3 Managerial Accounting (MA) — decisions inside the company

**What it is:** Internal analysis — costs, budgets, forecasts, variances — built to help **management decide**.

**Why it is called "managerial":** It is literally *for managers* — people who allocate resources and make choices. Not for the tax department.

**Orientation:** Future. Next quarter. Next product launch.

**Audience:** Internal — you, your leadership team, department heads.

**Rules:** **None mandatory.** No GAAP. No IFRS. You format the report to fit the decision. A cost breakdown for a make-vs-buy choice looks different from a budget variance report.

**Why MA is not governed by GAAP/IFRS:** External standards assume the reader is an outsider who cannot ask follow-up questions. You *can* ask your team anything. You need speed and relevance, not audit-grade uniformity.

**Typical outputs:**
- Cost analysis reports
- Budget vs actual (variance) reports
- Product profitability analysis
- Break-even estimates
- Forecasts and scenarios

**What MA is NOT:** A substitute for audited books. You cannot send your internal budget to a bank and call it financial statements.

---

### 3.4 Side-by-side comparison

| Dimension | Financial Accounting (FA) | Managerial Accounting (MA) |
|---|---|---|
| Time focus | Past | Future |
| Primary users | External (bank, investor, tax) | Internal (you, your team) |
| Rules | GAAP / IFRS — mandatory | Flexible — your choice |
| Report frequency | Fixed periods (quarterly, annual) | Whenever management needs it |
| Purpose | Report and comply | Decide and act |
| Example report | Audited Income Statement | Budget variance report |
| Can include estimates? | Only within accounting rules | Yes — forecasts are the point |

**The line that captures everything:**  
**MA focuses on the future; FA mainly reports past performance.**

---

### 3.5 Why you need both as CEO

| Your question | Use |
|---|---|
| Can we get a bank loan? | FA — they want audited, standardized history |
| Should we buy Slack? | MA — internal cost/benefit, forward-looking |
| Are we profitable? | FA (P&L) — but ask about cash too |
| Why did manufacturing cost exceed budget by 12%? | MA — variance analysis |
| Can an investor compare us to a competitor? | FA — standardized principles |
| Should we discontinue a product line? | MA — contribution and cost per line |

**Using only FA for internal decisions:** Too slow, backward-looking, wrong tool.  
**Using only MA for banks and investors:** They will not accept it. They need audited FA.

---

### 3.6 Related terms you will hear

**Bookkeeping:** The day-to-day recording of transactions. Subset of accounting. Your accountant's team does this.

**Auditing:** Independent checking that FA reports follow the rules. Not the same as either FA or MA — it verifies FA.

**Tax accounting:** How transactions are treated for tax law. Overlaps with FA but has its own rules. Your CA handles filing; you handle whether the business model makes sense.

**Cost accounting:** A branch of MA focused on understanding costs — especially relevant for manufacturing and product businesses. Shows up again in Chapter 3.

---

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

### The CEO's decision lens

When someone hands you a number or asks for one, run this filter:

```
STEP 1: Who is the audience?
  → External (bank, investor, regulator)     → Financial Accounting
  → Internal (you, your team)                → Managerial Accounting

STEP 2: What time period?
  → Already happened, must be verified       → Financial Accounting
  → Forecast, budget, "what if"              → Managerial Accounting

STEP 3: Is comparability required?
  → Must compare to other companies          → Financial Accounting
  → Specific to your decision only           → Managerial Accounting
```

### Report identification quick guide

| Report | FA or MA? |
|---|---|
| Audited Income Statement | FA |
| Balance Sheet | FA |
| Cash Flow Statement | FA |
| Budget Variance Report | MA |
| Product Cost Analysis | MA |
| Break-even estimate before launch | MA |
| Internal forecast of next year's production costs | MA |
| Last year's profit summary for shareholders | FA |

## 5. WORKED EXAMPLE

**Company:** BuildRight (12 employees, B2B SaaS)

**Request 1 — Bank loan**  
The bank wants two years of audited financial statements.

- **Type:** Financial Accounting  
- **Why:** External user, standardized format, historical performance, credit risk assessment  
- **What you do:** Hand them the audited P&L, Balance Sheet, and Cash Flow your CA prepared. You do not hand them your internal budget.

**Request 2 — Cloud server budget**  
Ops wants ₹40,000/month for new infrastructure next quarter.

- **Type:** Managerial Accounting  
- **Why:** Internal decision, forward-looking, customized to your ops plan  
- **What you do:** Compare budget to expected benefit (uptime, speed, customer churn). This is not a GAAP report — it is a management decision memo.

**Request 3 — Claude + Slack ROI**  
Ma'am asks: total cost, expected benefit, ROI, payback months.

- **Type:** Managerial Accounting  
- **Why:** Internal tool purchase decision, requires estimates and forecasts  
- **What you do:** Build a simple cost-benefit model (Chapter 5 covers this in full).

**Request 4 — VC due diligence**  
A venture capitalist asks for audited financials before term sheet.

- **Type:** Financial Accounting  
- **Why:** External investor, needs standardized, reliable, comparable reports  
- **Not useful to the VC:** Your internal production schedules or employee performance dashboards.

**Two reports on the same CEO's desk:**

| Report | Content | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Report A: Predicts next year's production costs | Forward-looking internal forecast | **Managerial Accounting** |
| Report B: Summarizes last year's profits for shareholders | Historical, external-facing | **Financial Accounting** |

# CHAPTER 2: How Do I Tell If My Company Is Actually Making Money?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

**Company:** TableCraft — you buy wooden tables at ₹1,000 each and sell them at ₹1,500 each.

Year 1: You buy 10 tables, sell 6. Your accountant says you made a **loss**. Your gut says you made **profit** — you sold 6 tables at ₹500 margin each, so ₹3,000 profit. Who is right?

Year 2: You sell 4 more tables for ₹6,000 total. Your accountant says profit is only ₹2,000. You expected ₹6,000. What happened?

Meanwhile, your bank statement shows you are **running out of cash** even though the P&L says you are profitable. A supplier calls — payment is overdue. Your investor asks for "the financials." Your ops lead asks if you can afford to hire.

You need three tools — and you need to understand that they answer **different questions**.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Lamba's central warning: **"Never confuse money with profit!"**

Most founders — and many experienced managers — assume:
- Revenue growth = success
- Profit = money in the bank
- What the P&L says = what you can spend

All three assumptions can be wrong.

**Why this matters for you as CEO:**
- You can double revenue and still lose money (rising costs eat the top line).
- You can show profit and still not pay suppliers (cash is stuck in inventory or unpaid invoices).
- You can read financial statements without knowing how to prepare them — Lamba is explicit about this. Your job is judgment, not bookkeeping.

**The primary objective of a business is to earn profit** — but Lamba adds a second pillar: **the ability to manage cash flow.** Winning half the battle (profit) is not enough if you lose the other half (cash).

---

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 Where do financial statements come from?

Every financial transaction — a sale, a salary payment, buying inventory, taking a loan — gets recorded in **accounting records** (the books).

Those records are summarized into two primary statements:

| Statement | Question it answers | Time dimension |
|---|---|---|
| **Profit & Loss Account (P&L)** / **Income Statement** | Did we make or lose money? | **Period** — a month, quarter, year |
| **Balance Sheet** | What do we own and what do we owe? | **Point in time** — a snapshot on one date |

A third statement connects them to cash:

| Statement | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Cash Flow Statement** | Where did cash actually come from and go? |

---

### 3.2 Profit & Loss Account (Income Statement)

**What it is:** A summary of **income** minus **expenses** over a period.

**Why "Profit & Loss":** It shows whether the business made a **profit** (income exceeded expenses) or a **loss** (expenses exceeded income).

**Why "Income Statement":** Same document, different name. "Income" is the accounting word for money earned from operations (primarily sales).

**The basic formula:**

```
Profit = Income − Expenses
Loss   = Expenses − Income    (when expenses are greater)
```

**Top Line vs Bottom Line — why those names?**

On a standard P&L, layout drives the jargon:

| Term | What it means | Where on P&L |
|---|---|---|
| **Top Line** | Total revenue / sales | First major number at the top |
| **Bottom Line** | Net profit after all expenses | Last number at the bottom |

Lamba's warning: *"Seeing the sales figure grow could create a misplaced feeling of success unless it is also accompanied by growing profit."*

Growing the top line without growing the bottom line means you are working harder, not smarter.

**What belongs on the P&L:**
- **Income** — primarily from **sales** (core business). "Other income" exists but sustainable businesses earn from core operations.
- **Expenses** — costs **consumed** in this period (we unpack "consumed" in Chapter 3).

**What does NOT belong on the P&L:**
- Assets you still own (unsold inventory, equipment, cash in bank as a balance)
- Loans you took (that affects Balance Sheet, not P&L directly)

---

### 3.3 The critical distinction: Expenditure vs Expense

This is where most founders get fooled — by their own intuition or by an accountant who does not explain.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Expenditure** | Any money you **spent** — the broadest term |
| **Expense** | The portion of expenditure **consumed** in this period |

**Why this matters:**  
Profit = Income − **Expenses** — NOT Income − **Expenditure**.

If you spend ₹10,00,000 buying tables but only sell half, you did not "expense" ₹10,00,000. Part of that spending is still sitting on your shelf as inventory — an **asset**, not an expense.

Lamba: *"The ultimate test is consumption."*

**The consumption test — two questions:**
1. Was this spending necessary to generate income?
2. Was it **fully consumed** in this period?

- **Yes, fully consumed** → **Expense** → hits P&L, reduces profit  
- **No, not fully consumed** → **Asset** → hits Balance Sheet, does not reduce profit yet

**Depreciation — why this word exists:**  
When you buy equipment that lasts years, you do not expense it all in month one. **Depreciation** is the portion of that asset "used up" each period, recorded as expense. From Latin *de-* (away) + *pretium* (price) — literally writing down the asset's price over time.

---

### 3.4 Balance Sheet

**What it is:** A snapshot of **assets** (what you own) and **liabilities** (what you owe) on a specific date.

**Why "Balance Sheet":**  
In **double-entry bookkeeping**, every transaction affects two accounts equally. Debits always equal credits. So:

```
Assets = Liabilities (+ Owner's Equity)
```

The sheet must **balance**. That is not a suggestion — it is how the system is built.

**Why "Balance":** From the requirement that both sides balance.  
**Why "Sheet":** Historical term for a tabular financial report.

**Lamba's analogy:** *"Financial transactions flow through an organization like water in a river. An instant snapshot of all this financial activity gives the Balance Sheet for that particular moment."*

**Assets — what you own:**
- **Fixed assets** — long-lived (equipment, vehicles, office)
- **Current assets** — short-term (cash, inventory, money customers owe you)

**Why "asset":** From Latin — property with future economic value.

**Liabilities — what you owe:**
- To **owners** (capital invested)
- To **external creditors** (loans, suppliers you have not paid yet)

**Why "liability":** From Latin *ligare* — to bind. Obligations that bind the company.

**Four categories — all accounting data falls into one:**

| Category | Appears on |
|---|---|
| Income | P&L |
| Expenses | P&L |
| Assets | Balance Sheet |
| Liabilities | Balance Sheet |

**Double-entry — why "debit" and "credit" confuse everyone:**

Every transaction hits **two accounts** with equal and opposite effects.

| Term | Origin | In accounting |
|---|---|---|
| **Debit** | Latin *debere* — to owe | One side of a ledger entry |
| **Credit** | Latin *credere* — to believe, trust | The other side |

**Warning for CEOs:** Banking uses "credit" to mean money IN. Accounting uses debit and credit as neutral labels for two sides of an entry. When your accountant says "we credited inventory," they are not saying money arrived — they are describing which side of the ledger entry was affected. You do not need to master debits and credits to read statements. You need to understand **what increased and what decreased**.

**Example — borrow ₹100:**
- Cash (asset) increases by ₹100
- Loan (liability) increases by ₹100  
- Balance Sheet still balances: Assets = Liabilities

---

### 3.5 Cash Flow Statement

**What it is:** Tracks **actual cash** moving in and out over a period.

**Why it exists separately from P&L:**  
The P&L uses **accrual accounting** — you record revenue when **earned**, expenses when **incurred**, even if cash has not moved yet.

**Examples of profit vs cash disconnect:**
- You sold ₹5,00,000 on credit — revenue on P&L, no cash yet
- You bought ₹3,00,000 of inventory in cash — cash out, but unsold stock is an asset not fully expensed
- You show depreciation expense on P&L — reduces profit, but no cash left the bank

Lamba: *"Only half the battle has been won when you are able to make profit. The other half will be won if you are also able to generate cash."*

**Profits do not represent money.** A profitable company can have a **negative bank balance**.

---

### 3.6 How the three statements connect

```
ACCOUNTING RECORDS (every transaction)
        │
        ├──► P&L (period):     Income − Expenses = Profit
        │
        ├──► Balance Sheet (snapshot):  Assets = Liabilities
        │
        └──► Cash Flow (period):  Actual cash in − cash out

CONSUMPTION TEST links P&L and Balance Sheet:
  Unconsumed spending → Asset (Balance Sheet)
  Consumed spending   → Expense (P&L)
```

**CEO reading order when something feels wrong:**
1. **P&L** — Are we profitable on paper?
2. **Cash Flow** — Is cash actually moving?
3. **Balance Sheet** — Where is value stuck? (Inventory? Receivables? Debt?)

---

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

### Reading a P&L — what to look for

```
REVENUE (Top Line)                    ₹XX,XX,XXX
− Cost of goods sold / operating costs  ₹XX,XX,XXX
= GROSS PROFIT                          ₹XX,XX,XXX
− Operating expenses (rent, salaries)   ₹XX,XX,XXX
= OPERATING PROFIT                      ₹XX,XX,XXX
− Interest, taxes, other                ₹XX,XX,XXX
= NET PROFIT (Bottom Line)              ₹XX,XX,XXX
```

**Three questions to ask:**
1. Is revenue growing?
2. Is the bottom line growing **with** revenue?
3. Where are the biggest expense lines — and are they necessary?

### Reading a Balance Sheet — what to look for

```
ASSETS                          LIABILITIES
Fixed assets (equipment)        Owner's capital
Current assets:                 Long-term loans
  Cash                          Current liabilities:
  Inventory                       Supplier dues
  Receivables                     Short-term debt
```

**Three questions to ask:**
1. How much cash do we actually have?
2. How much is locked in inventory or unpaid invoices?
3. How much do we owe, and when is it due?

### Reading Cash Flow — what to look for

Three sections:
- **Operating** — cash from running the business
- **Investing** — cash spent on or received from assets
- **Financing** — loans, equity, repayments

**The one question:** Is operating cash flow positive? If P&L shows profit but operating cash flow is negative, find out where cash is trapped.

## 5. WORKED EXAMPLE

### TableCraft — Year 1 (the profit puzzle)

**Unit economics:**
- Buy price: ₹1,000 per table
- Sell price: ₹1,500 per table
- Margin per table sold: ₹500

**Activity:**
- Purchased: 10 tables → ₹10,000 cash out
- Sold: 6 tables → ₹9,000 revenue
- Unsold: 4 tables still in stock

**Wrong method (treating all purchases as expense):**

| | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Income | 9,000 |
| Expense (all 10 tables purchased) | 10,000 |
| **Result** | **LOSS of 1,000** |

**Correct method (only consumed goods are expense):**

| | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Income | 9,000 |
| Cost of goods sold (6 tables × ₹1,000) | 6,000 |
| **Profit** | **3,000** |
| Unsold inventory (asset on Balance Sheet) | 4,000 |

Your gut was right. The accountant's "loss" was a **classification error** — expensing inventory you still own.

---

### TableCraft — Year 2 (opening inventory)

**Activity:**
- Opening inventory: 4 tables worth ₹4,000 (carried from Year 1)
- Sold: 4 tables for ₹6,000 (4 × ₹1,500)
- No new purchases

| | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Revenue | 6,000 |
| Cost of goods sold (4 tables consumed from opening stock) | 4,000 |
| **Profit** | **2,000** |

You received ₹6,000 cash but profit is only ₹2,000 — because ₹4,000 of that revenue replaces the cost of tables you already paid for in Year 1. **Profit follows consumption, not cash received.**

---

### TableCraft — the cash trap (furniture store version)

Lamba's larger example:

| Event | Amount |
|---|---|
| Buy 100 tables at ₹1,000 | ₹1,00,000 cash OUT |
| Sell 50 tables at ₹1,500 | ₹75,000 cash IN |
| Unsold inventory (50 tables) | ₹50,000 locked in stock |

**P&L may show profit** (revenue ₹75,000 minus COGS on 50 units ₹50,000 = ₹25,000 profit).

**Bank reality:** You spent ₹1,00,000 and received ₹75,000. You are **₹25,000 poorer in cash** and still holding ₹50,000 of unsold stock.

**This is the "profitable but broke" problem.**

---

### Balance Sheet snapshot — two companies, same assets

Lenders look at structure, not just profit:

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Share Capital (owner money) | 450 | 150 |
| Long-term Loans | 200 | 650 |
| Current Liabilities | 50 | 100 |
| **Total Liabilities** | **700** | **900** |
| Fixed Assets | 750 | 750 |
| Current Assets | 250 | 250 |
| **Total Assets** | **1000** | **1000** |
| Owner contribution % | **75%** | **15%** |

Same total assets. Company A is mostly owner-funded. Company B is mostly debt-funded. **A bank prefers A** — more owner skin in the game, lower lender risk.

## 6. YOUR TURN

**Company:** FreshBite — meal prep delivery, 6 months old.

**Given data for last month:**
- Revenue (sales): ₹8,00,000
- Ingredients purchased (cash paid): ₹5,00,000
- Ingredients still in fridge (unused): ₹1,00,000 worth
- Salaries paid: ₹2,00,000
- Rent paid: ₹80,000
- Customers owe you (not yet paid): ₹1,50,000

**Calculate and answer:**

1. What is **income** for the month? (Hint: accrual — include what you earned, not just cash received)  
2. What are **expenses** for the month? (Hint: only consumed costs)  
3. What is **profit**?  
4. Why might you struggle to pay suppliers even if profit looks healthy?  
5. Where does the unused ₹1,00,000 of ingredients appear — P&L or Balance Sheet?

**Answer key:**

1. **Income = ₹8,00,000** (sales earned; receivables are income even if cash not yet received).  
2. **Expenses = ₹4,00,000 ingredients consumed (5L purchased − 1L unused) + ₹2,00,000 salaries + ₹80,000 rent = ₹6,80,000**.  
3. **Profit = ₹8,00,000 − ₹6,80,000 = ₹1,20,000**.  
4. Cash may be tied up: you paid ₹5L for ingredients + ₹2L salaries + ₹80K rent = ₹7.8L out, but only received cash from customers who already paid (₹8L − ₹1.5L receivable = ₹6.5L cash from sales). You can be profitable with tight cash.  
5. **Balance Sheet** — unused ingredients are inventory (asset), not expense.

# CHAPTER 3: How Do I Calculate What Something Really Costs?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

**Company:** PackRight — you manufacture custom shipping boxes for e-commerce brands.

Three decisions land this week:

1. **Make vs buy:** A vendor offers to manufacture boxes at ₹12/unit. Your ops team says in-house cost is "around ₹10." Your accountant's P&L shows total manufacturing costs of ₹8,00,000 last month. Which number do you trust?

2. **Pricing:** A big client wants 50,000 boxes at ₹14/unit. Your usual price is ₹18. Your sales head says "revenue is revenue." You are not sure.

3. **Cash crunch:** You are profitable on paper but payroll is tight. Your CA says costs are "under control." You do not believe it.

All three require the same foundation: **knowing what something really costs** — not the sticker price, not the bank outflow, not the P&L total blindly.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Founders routinely confuse four different "costs":

| What people say | What they often mean | What you actually need |
|---|---|---|
| "We spent ₹X" | Cash that left the bank | Cash cost |
| "It's on the P&L" | Expense recognized this period | Consumed cost |
| "Fully loaded cost" | Everything allocated to a product | Full cost (for pricing long-term) |
| "Cost of one more unit" | — | Marginal / variable cost (for specific decisions) |

Using the wrong cost for the wrong decision is how businesses **lose money while growing revenue**.

Lamba's line worth tattooing on your brain: **"It is consumption and not spending which should be considered as an expense."**

And for decisions: **"Profit is an opinion; cash in the bank is a fact."**

---

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 The three layers of "true cost"

As CEO, you need three layers — each answers a different question:

| Layer | Question | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Cash cost** | What left the bank? | Bank statement, cash flow |
| **2. Consumed cost** | What was used up to earn this period's revenue? | P&L, COGS, consumption test |
| **3. Decision cost** | What changes if we do X vs Y? | Variable cost, contribution, break-even |

Chapter 2 covered layers 1 and 2. This chapter builds layer 3 — and connects all three.

---

### 3.2 Fixed costs vs variable costs — why the distinction exists

Not all costs behave the same when you sell more or less.

| Type | Behavior | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Variable costs (VC)** | Rise and fall **with volume** | Raw materials per box, packaging per unit, payment processing %, sales commission per sale |
| **Fixed costs (FC)** | Stay the same **in short run** regardless of volume | Rent, core salaried team, insurance, software subscriptions |

**Why "variable":** They **vary** with output.  
**Why "fixed":** They are **fixed** in place for the period — you pay rent whether you sell 100 boxes or 10,000.

**Semi-variable costs:** Utilities, overtime — part fixed, part variable. For decisions, split them as best you can.

**Why this matters for startups:**  
- High fixed costs = high upside when sales grow, **high danger** when sales drop.  
- Early-stage companies often prefer **variable** costs (freelancers, cloud, commission) until demand is predictable.  
- Every hire, lease, or equipment purchase **raises your break-even** — the sales you need just to stop losing money.

---

### 3.3 Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) — why "sold" is in the name

**COGS** = the cost of inventory **consumed** (sold or used) in the period.

**Formula:**

```
COGS = Opening Inventory + Purchases − Closing Inventory
```

**Why not just "purchases"?**  
Because buying stock is not the same as using it. Unsold inventory is an asset, not yet a cost of sales.

**TableCraft recap:**

| Year | Purchases | Closing stock | COGS | Logic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₹10,000 (10 tables) | ₹4,000 (4 unsold) | ₹6,000 | Only 6 sold |
| 2 | ₹0 | ₹0 | ₹4,000 | Used opening stock |

---

### 3.4 Contribution — why this word?

Once you separate variable from fixed costs, a new number appears:

```
Contribution = Sales − Variable Costs
```

**Why "contribution"?**  
Lamba: *"Contribution is called Contribution because it is the contribution of each unit sold towards the organization's bottom line."*

Each sale **contributes** first toward covering fixed costs. Only after fixed costs are covered does each sale add **profit**.

```
Profit = Contribution − Fixed Costs
```

**Why this is more useful than "profit" alone for decisions:**  
Contribution tells you whether **one more sale** helps or hurts — regardless of how you allocate fixed costs across products.

---

### 3.5 Full cost vs marginal cost — two different questions

| Concept | Question | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **Full cost** | What does it cost to make and sustain this product? | Long-term pricing, profitability analysis |
| **Marginal (variable) cost** | What does **one more unit** cost? | Accept/reject an order, make vs buy, discount decisions |

**Critical insight from Lamba:**  
You can rationally sell **below full cost** but **above variable cost** if fixed costs are already committed for the period. You still **lose money overall**, but you lose **less** than shutting down — because each sale contributes something toward fixed costs.

**Wrong:** "Price must cover full cost per unit on every order."  
**Right:** "Does this price exceed variable cost and serve a strategic purpose?"

---

### 3.6 Make vs buy — what "cost" means in this decision

Outsourcing vs in-house is not answered by the Income Statement total. You need a **Cost Analysis Report** — a MA tool that compares:

| Cost element | In-house | Outsource |
|---|---|---|
| Direct materials per unit | ? | Included in vendor price |
| Direct labor per unit | ? | — |
| Allocated overhead | ? | — |
| Vendor price per unit | — | ? |
| Quality control / shipping | ? | ? |
| Flexibility / lead time | Qualitative | Qualitative |

The P&L shows **total** manufacturing cost. It does not automatically split what changes if you outsource.

---

### 3.7 Inventory — the cost trap that fakes profit

**Why inventory distorts cost:**

Accountants often record **purchases when goods are bought** (practical bookkeeping). Economically, only **sold/consumed** goods are this period's cost.

**The danger:**  
Higher closing inventory → lower recognized expense → **higher reported profit** — while **cash already left** to buy stock.

**Royal Dutch Shell** (mentioned by Lamba) inflated inventory values to overstate profit. You may not commit fraud — but you can fool yourself by bulk-buying inventory and feeling profitable.

**CEO rule:** Ask not just "what's profit?" but **"how much cash is sitting in inventory?"**

---

### 3.8 Working capital — the cost of running day to day

**Working capital** = money tied up in daily operations.

```
Gross Working Capital  = Current Assets (cash + inventory + receivables)
Net Working Capital    = Current Assets − Current Liabilities
```

**Why it matters for cost:**  
Supplier credit reduces immediate cash need — but if you rely too heavily on short-term debt to fund operations, your **current ratio** falls and banks see risk.

**Mini example (Lamba):**

| Item | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Fixed Assets | 80 |
| Current Assets | 20 |
| Current Liabilities | 10 |
| **Net Working Capital** | **10** |

You need ₹10 of long-term funding to sustain day-to-day operations after supplier credit.

**Cost you feel:** If ₹15 of your cash is in slow-moving inventory, that is ₹15 not available for payroll — even if P&L looks fine.

---

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

### Framework 1 — Consumption test (from Ch 2, applied to cost)

```
Money spent
    │
    ├── Fully consumed this period?  → Expense / COGS  → reduces profit
    │
    └── Not fully consumed?        → Asset (inventory, equipment)  → Balance Sheet
                                          │
                                          └── Used over time?  → Depreciation expense each period
```

### Framework 2 — Contribution and break-even

```
Contribution per unit = Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit

Break-even units = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit

Break-even sales (₹) = Break-even units × Selling Price
```

**Break-even** = the point where Contribution exactly covers Fixed Costs. No profit, no loss. Named because you "break even" — you stop bleeding.

**Profit at any volume:**

```
Profit = (Units sold × Contribution per unit) − Fixed Costs
```

**PV Ratio (Profit-Volume Ratio):**

```
PV Ratio = Contribution ÷ Sales
```

Shows what percentage of each rupee of sales becomes contribution. Useful for comparing product lines.

### Framework 3 — Decision checklist

| Decision | Cost measure to use |
|---|---|
| Monthly profitability | P&L with correct COGS (consumed cost) |
| Can we pay bills? | Cash flow + inventory + receivables |
| Accept discount bulk order? | Contribution per unit (variable cost vs offered price) |
| Outsource production? | Cost Analysis Report (full comparison) |
| What must we sell to survive? | Break-even units |
| Is growth helping? | Inventory days + net working capital trend |

## 5. WORKED EXAMPLE

### PackRight — Unit economics and break-even

**Given:**
- Selling price per box: ₹18
- Variable cost per box (materials + direct labor + shipping): ₹11
- Fixed costs per month (rent, salaried staff, machinery depreciation): ₹2,80,000

**Step 1 — Contribution per unit**

```
Contribution = ₹18 − ₹11 = ₹7 per box
```

**Step 2 — Break-even volume**

```
Break-even units = ₹2,80,000 ÷ ₹7 = 40,000 boxes/month
```

PackRight must sell **40,000 boxes** just to cover fixed costs.

**Step 3 — Profit at 55,000 boxes**

```
Total contribution = 55,000 × ₹7 = ₹3,85,000
Profit = ₹3,85,000 − ₹2,80,000 = ₹1,05,000
```

**Step 4 — The discount order decision**

Big client offers 50,000 boxes at **₹14/unit** (below your ₹18 price).

| | Per unit |
|---|---|
| Offered price | ₹14 |
| Variable cost | ₹11 |
| **Contribution** | **₹3** |

Full cost per box (if you allocate fixed costs): ₹11 + (₹2,80,000 ÷ current volume). At 40,000 units, fixed allocation = ₹7/box → "full cost" ≈ ₹18.

**Analysis:**  
- ₹14 is **below full cost** — looks like a loss.  
- ₹14 is **above variable cost** (₹11) — each box still contributes ₹3 toward fixed costs.  
- 50,000 × ₹3 = ₹1,50,000 contribution from this order.

**If fixed costs are already committed this month:** Accepting may be rational — you are better off contributing ₹1,50,000 toward rent and salaries than leaving capacity idle.

**If this becomes your only customer at ₹14:** You renegotiate or restructure fixed costs — long-term pricing must cover full cost.

---

### PackRight — Make vs buy

| | In-house (₹/unit) | Vendor (₹/unit) |
|---|---|---|
| Materials | 6 | — |
| Direct labor | 3 | — |
| Variable overhead (power, wear) | 2 | — |
| **Variable cost** | **11** | **12** |
| Allocated fixed cost (at 40K volume) | 7 | — |
| **Full cost** | **18** | **12** |

**Short-term (capacity available):** Vendor at ₹12 vs in-house variable ₹11 — **in-house is ₹1 cheaper per unit** on marginal basis.

**Long-term (at capacity, need new machine):** Adding fixed cost of new equipment changes break-even. Run the full Cost Analysis Report including capex — not just per-unit sticker price.

---

### PackRight — Inventory cash trap

| Event | P&L effect | Cash effect |
|---|---|---|
| Buy ₹6,00,000 raw cardboard (cash) | Not fully expensed if unused | **−₹6,00,000 cash now** |
| Manufacture and sell boxes with ₹4,00,000 of materials | COGS ₹4,00,000 | Cash already spent |
| ₹2,00,000 materials still in warehouse | Asset on Balance Sheet | **₹2,00,000 locked** |

Profit looks fine. Cash is tight. **Cost of growth** includes cash tied in inventory — not just P&L expense.

## 6. YOUR TURN

**Company:** CodeServe — IT services, 10 employees.

**Data:**
- Monthly fixed costs (salaries, office, tools): ₹6,00,000
- Variable cost per project day (contractor + licenses): ₹8,000
- You charge clients ₹15,000 per project day

**Questions:**

1. What is contribution per project day?  
2. How many project days per month to break even?  
3. You sold 90 project days last month. What was profit?  
4. A client offers a 6-month contract at ₹10,000 per day (20 days/month). Variable cost unchanged. Accept or reject? Why?  
5. Your P&L shows ₹2,00,000 profit but bank balance dropped. Name two places cash could be trapped.

**Answer key:**

1. **₹15,000 − ₹8,000 = ₹7,000 contribution per day**  
2. **₹6,00,000 ÷ ₹7,000 ≈ 86 project days**  
3. **(90 × ₹7,000) − ₹6,00,000 = ₹6,30,000 − ₹6,00,000 = ₹30,000 profit**

5. **Unpaid receivables** (clients owe you) and **prepaid expenses / deposits** — or slow collections on the 6-month contract.

**Question 6**  
A company is deciding whether to outsource production or manufacture in-house. Which accounting information would be most relevant?

A) Income Statement  
B) Cost Analysis Report  
C) Balance Sheet  
D) Statement of Cash Flows  

**Answer: B — Cost Analysis Report**

Make-vs-buy needs detailed comparison of costs for each option — materials, labor, overhead, shipping, quality. A Cost Analysis Report breaks this down.
