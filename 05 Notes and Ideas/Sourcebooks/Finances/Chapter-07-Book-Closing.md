# CHAPTER 7: How Do I Use All of This When Running My Startup?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

You are the founder of **NovaCraft** — a 10-person startup in Bengaluru that designs and sells modular home-office furniture to remote workers and small companies. Revenue is growing. So is the chaos.

Over the past year you learned seven separate skills: which numbers to trust (Chapter 1), whether you are actually making money (Chapter 2), what things really cost (Chapter 3), when you stop losing money (Chapter 4), how to justify a purchase with ROI (Chapter 5), and how to spot trouble before your accountant smooths it over (Chapter 6).

Now it is April. Your year ahead has four real decisions — one per quarter — and each one pulls a different tool off the shelf:

| Quarter | Decision on your desk |
|---|---|
| **Q1 (Apr–Jun)** | Launch a budget line called **NovaLite** at ₹9,500 per desk? |
| **Q2 (Jul–Sep)** | Buy **Claude + Slack** for the team — ma'am wants ROI in writing |
| **Q3 (Oct–Dec)** | Profit is down 38%. Cash is worse. What broke? |
| **Q4 (Jan–Mar)** | A seed investor wants audited books. Outsource assembly or buy equipment? |

None of these is "an accounting question." They are **CEO questions** that accounting helps you answer. This chapter shows you how to run all seven skills as one operating rhythm — not seven separate homework topics.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Founders who learn finance in pieces often freeze when real decisions arrive. They know break-even exists. They know ROI is a percentage. They cannot connect either to a Tuesday morning when payroll is due, a client wants a discount, and an investor emails asking for statements.

The gap is not knowledge. It is **integration**.

Anil Lamba's core message across *Romancing the Balance Sheet* is that financial management is not clerical — it is the intellectual work of connecting actions to outcomes. As CEO you are not the scorekeeper. You are the player who picks which shot to take. But you cannot play blind.

**Without integration, three failures repeat:**

1. **Wrong tool, wrong decision** — Using last year's audited P&L to decide whether to accept a discount order (FA when you need MA).
2. **Right answer, wrong timing** — Approving a tool purchase with strong ROI while cash is trapped in receivables and inventory.
3. **Trusting the headline** — Celebrating profit while working capital drains the bank account.

The chapters in this kit were deliberately sequenced: trust → statements → cost → break-even → ROI → early warning → **this chapter**. You are not learning accounting. You are building a decision reflex.

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 The CEO Decision Map

Every financial question you face maps to one of two systems and one of three question types.

**System filter (Chapter 1):**

| Audience | Time | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| External (bank, investor, tax) | Past, standardized | **Financial Accounting** — P&L, Balance Sheet, Cash Flow |
| Internal (you, your team) | Future, flexible | **Managerial Accounting** — cost analysis, break-even, ROI, variance |

**Question-type filter (Chapters 2–6):**

```
                    ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
                    │         WHAT ARE YOU ASKING?        │
                    └─────────────────────────────────────┘
                                      │
          ┌───────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┐
          ▼                           ▼                           ▼
   "Are we making            "Should we do X?"            "Something feels
    money on paper?"          (launch, buy, price)          wrong — why?"
          │                           │                           │
          ▼                           ▼                           ▼
   Ch 2: P&L + BS +          Ch 3–4: Cost +              Ch 6: Variance +
   Cash Flow                  Contribution +               Working capital +
   (profit ≠ cash)           Break-even + ROI             Profit vs cash
          │                           │                           │
          └───────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┘
                                      │
                                      ▼
                         Ch 7: Run all three monthly
```

**Decision-to-chapter quick map:**

| Decision type | Primary chapters | Key outputs |
|---|---|---|
| Loan, investment, compliance | Ch 1, 2 | Audited FA statements |
| Pricing, discount, new product | Ch 3, 4 | Contribution, break-even |
| Tool purchase, hire, capex | Ch 3, 5 | ROI, payback period |
| Profit falling, cash tight | Ch 2, 6 | Variance report, funds-flow, current ratio |
| Growth vs survival | Ch 3, 4, 6 | Break-even + working capital together |

No single report answers every question. The CEO's job is to know **which report answers which question** — then ask for it before deciding.

### 3.2 The Monthly CEO Rhythm

Accounting runs on periods. So should you. A 60-minute monthly rhythm beats a quarterly panic.

**Week 1 — Cash truth (15 min)**  
Open the bank statement. Not the P&L. Ask: *What is our cash runway at current burn?* If cash and profit diverge, flag it immediately (Chapter 2).

**Week 2 — Statement scan (20 min)**  
Review P&L and Balance Sheet your CA sends. Three numbers only:
- Revenue trend (top line)
- Net profit (bottom line)
- Cash from operations on Cash Flow Statement

Ask: *Do these three tell the same story?* If profit is up and operating cash is down, go to Chapter 6 before making new commitments.

**Week 3 — MA check (15 min)**  
One internal number: budget vs actual on your biggest cost line, or contribution per unit on your top product. This is variance analysis in miniature (Chapter 6). Catches problems while they are small.

**Week 4 — Decision queue (10 min)**  
List every pending decision. Tag each FA or MA using the filter from Chapter 1. Anything MA-tagged gets a one-page memo: cost, benefit, break-even or ROI, cash impact.

**Quarterly (half day):** Walk through all four decision types on the map. Update forecasts. This is when NovaCraft's quarterly decisions get made — not ad hoc in Slack threads.

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

### The Integrated Decision Protocol

For any decision, run five steps in order. Skipping a step is how smart founders make dumb bets.

**Step 1 — Classify**  
FA or MA? External or internal? Past or future?

**Step 2 — Pull the right numbers**  
| If deciding… | Pull… |
|---|---|
| Launch / price / discount | Variable cost, contribution, break-even |
| Buy / hire / invest | Total cost, expected benefit, payback, ROI |
| Explain performance | P&L + Cash Flow + variance |
| Raise money / borrow | Audited FA package |

**Step 3 — Run the math**  
Use the formulas from Chapters 3–5. Write it down. One page. Numbers, not vibes.

**Step 4 — Cash check**  
Even a great ROI fails if you cannot fund the upfront cost. Check bank balance, receivables, inventory, and payables (Chapter 6). **Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact.**

**Step 5 — Decide and log**  
Record the decision, the numbers used, and the review date. In three months you will variance-analyze your own forecast.

### The Quarterly Review Template

At each quarter-end, answer these four questions:

1. **Performance:** Did revenue and profit move as expected? (Ch 2)
2. **Unit economics:** Is contribution per unit still healthy? (Ch 3–4)
3. **Cash position:** Where is money trapped? (Ch 6)
4. **Pipeline:** What decisions are coming next quarter? Tag each FA/MA. (Ch 1)

## 5. WORKED EXAMPLE

### NovaCraft — Four Quarterly Decisions (Full INR Walkthrough)

**Baseline (start of year):**  
10 employees. Average selling price on core NovaDesk line: ₹14,500. Monthly fixed costs (rent, salaries, depreciation): ₹4,50,000. Variable cost per desk: ₹8,600. Cash in bank: ₹22,00,000.

---

### Q1 Decision: Launch NovaLite?

**Situation:** Product team proposes a simpler desk at ₹9,500 for students and gig workers. Additional fixed costs for the line: ₹1,80,000/month (part-time designer + targeted ads). Variable cost per NovaLite unit: ₹5,800.

**Tools used:** Chapters 3–4 (contribution, break-even)

```
Contribution per unit = ₹9,500 − ₹5,800 = ₹3,700

Break-even units = ₹1,80,000 ÷ ₹3,700 = 48.6 → 49 units/month
```

Marketing projects 70 units/month in Q1.

```
Projected contribution = 70 × ₹3,700 = ₹2,59,000
Projected profit from line = ₹2,59,000 − ₹1,80,000 = ₹79,000/month
```

**Cash check:** NovaLite uses ₹5,800 materials per unit. At 70 units, that's ₹4,06,000/month in variable cash outflow — manageable against ₹22L bank balance.

**Decision: GO.** Price exceeds variable cost. Volume clears break-even with margin. Log review for July.

---

### Q2 Decision: Claude + Slack for the team?

**Situation:** Ma'am asks for written ROI on AI and communication tools before Q2 budget lock.

**Tools used:** Chapters 1, 5 (MA classification, ROI, payback)

**Costs (annual):**

| Item | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Claude Pro | ₹2,000 × 12 users | ₹24,000 |
| Slack Business+ | ₹800 × 12 users | ₹9,600 |
| Setup + training | One-time | ₹60,000 |
| **Year 1 total cost** | | **₹93,600** |

**Benefit estimate:** Each team member saves 2 hours/week on drafting, searching files, and status updates. Loaded cost of time: ₹500/hour.

```
Hours saved/year = 2 hrs × 12 people × 48 weeks = 1,152 hours
Value of time saved = 1,152 × ₹500 = ₹5,76,000/year
```

**ROI (Year 1):**

```
Net benefit = ₹5,76,000 − ₹93,600 = ₹4,82,400

ROI = (Net benefit ÷ Total cost) × 100
    = (₹4,82,400 ÷ ₹93,600) × 100
    = 515%
```

**Payback:**

```
Monthly benefit = ₹5,76,000 ÷ 12 = ₹48,000
Payback = ₹93,600 ÷ ₹48,000 = 1.95 months ≈ 2 months
```

**Cash check:** ₹93,600 Year 1 cost is less than 5% of bank balance. Upfront ₹60,000 setup is affordable.

**Decision: APPROVE.** Strong MA case. This is not an FA decision — no auditor needs to sign off. Revisit benefit assumptions in Q4.

---

### Q3 Decision: Profit down, cash worse — what broke?

**Situation:** October board update. P&L shows profit of ₹4,20,000 for the quarter — down from ₹6,80,000 last quarter. Bank balance dropped ₹8,00,000. Your CA says "seasonal dip." You are not convinced.

**Tools used:** Chapters 2, 6 (profit vs cash, variance, working capital)

**Variance analysis (materials — biggest cost line):**

| | Budget | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material cost | ₹9,00,000 | ₹10,62,000 | **+18% unfavourable** |

Plywood prices rose. Nobody updated pricing. Contribution per core NovaDesk unit fell from ₹5,900 to ₹4,700.

**Funds-flow / balance sheet reading:**

| Item | Last quarter | This quarter | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | ₹18,00,000 | ₹10,00,000 | −₹8,00,000 |
| Receivables | ₹6,00,000 | ₹18,00,000 | +₹12,00,000 |
| Inventory | ₹4,00,000 | ₹9,00,000 | +₹5,00,000 |
| Payables | ₹5,00,000 | ₹7,00,000 | +₹2,00,000 |

**Current ratio:**

```
Current Assets = ₹10,00,000 + ₹18,00,000 + ₹9,00,000 = ₹37,00,000
Current Liabilities = ₹7,00,000
Current Ratio = ₹37,00,000 ÷ ₹7,00,000 = 5.3
```

Ratio looks healthy. **This is the credibility trap** (Chapter 6): receivables and inventory inflate current assets, masking a cash crisis. Profit says ₹4.2L earned. Cash says ₹8L left — because ₹12L is stuck in a corporate client's 60-day payment terms and ₹5L sits in unsold NovaLite stock.

**Actions:**
1. Renegotiate client terms to 30 days or require 50% advance.
2. Slow NovaLite production to match sell-through.
3. Pass 8% of material cost increase into NovaDesk pricing next month.
4. Weekly cash forecast until receivables normalise.

**Decision: DO NOT launch any new spend.** Fix working capital first. ROI on new tools means nothing if payroll bounces.

---

### Q4 Decision: Seed round + outsource vs own assembly?

**Situation:** A seed investor wants audited FY statements and a 12-month plan. You must also decide: outsource assembly (₹2,200/desk variable) or buy equipment (₹25,00,000 upfront, in-house variable drops to ₹1,750/desk). Projected volume: 800 desks/year.

**Tools used:** Chapters 1, 2, 3 (FA for investor, make vs buy, cash runway)

**FA package for investor (summary):**

| Statement | Key figure |
|---|---|
| P&L — Revenue | ₹1,20,00,000 |
| P&L — Net profit | ₹18,00,000 (15% margin) |
| Cash Flow — Operating activities | **(₹4,00,000)** negative |
| Balance Sheet — Net working capital | Tight after Q3; improving with collection fixes |

Investor sees profitable company with a cash conversion problem — honest FA disclosure builds trust (Chapter 1). You pair it with an MA plan showing Q3 fixes.

**Make vs buy at 800 units/year:**

| | Outsource | In-house (new equipment) |
|---|---|---|
| Variable cost/unit | ₹2,200 | ₹1,750 |
| Annual variable | ₹17,60,000 | ₹14,00,000 |
| Upfront capex | ₹0 | ₹25,00,000 |
| **Year 1 total cash** | **₹17,60,000** | **₹39,00,000** |

In-house saves ₹3,60,000/year in variable cost but ties up ₹25L that funds 14 months of fixed costs at current burn.

**Cash check:** Post-Q3 recovery, bank balance is ₹14,00,000. Equipment purchase would leave ₹(11,00,000) before the round closes.

**Decision: Outsource for 12 months.** Revisit in-house when (a) volume exceeds 1,500 units/year, (b) seed round closes, (c) operating cash flow is positive for two consecutive quarters. Send investor audited statements (FA) plus the outsource plan with break-even at higher volume (MA).

## 6. YOUR TURN

**Company:** FreshBite — meal-prep delivery, 8 employees, Pune.

**Quarterly decisions:**

1. **Q1:** New ₹299 lunch box. Variable cost ₹185. Extra fixed ₹95,000/month. Projected 420 boxes/month. Launch?
2. **Q2:** Delivery bikes on lease — ₹8,000/bike/month × 4 bikes vs hiring riders on commission (₹40/delivery, 600 deliveries/month). Which is cheaper on variable basis?
3. **Q3:** Profit ₹1,80,000 but cash down ₹3,50,000. Receivables up ₹2,80,000, raw food inventory up ₹1,20,000. Name the problem and one action.
4. **Q4:** Cloud kitchen investor wants last year's P&L and Cash Flow. Your ops lead sends a budget forecast instead. Correct that mistake in one sentence.

**Answer key:**

1. Contribution = ₹299 − ₹185 = ₹114. Break-even = ₹95,000 ÷ ₹114 = 833 boxes. At 420 boxes, line loses money. **Do not launch** unless volume forecast doubles or fixed cost drops.
2. Lease = ₹32,000/month fixed. Commission = 600 × ₹40 = ₹24,000 variable. Commission is cheaper at 600 deliveries. Revisit at 800+ deliveries when lease may win.
3. **Working capital trap** — profit on paper, cash stuck in receivables and perishable inventory. Action: collect outstanding payments within 7 days; reduce raw stock to 3-day cover.
4. **Investor needs FA (audited historical P&L and Cash Flow), not an MA budget forecast.** Send the right document.

---

**Question A**  
NovaCraft's founder receives audited financial statements for a seed investor meeting and, the same week, builds a break-even model for a new product line. Which pairing is correct?

A) Both are Financial Accounting  
B) Investor statements = FA; break-even model = MA  
C) Both are Managerial Accounting  
D) Investor statements = MA; break-even model = FA  

**Answer: B** — External, historical, standardized reports are FA. Internal, forward-looking break-even analysis is MA.

---

**Question B**  
NovaCraft shows ₹4,20,000 quarterly profit but cash fell ₹8,00,000. Receivables rose ₹12,00,000 and inventory rose ₹5,00,000. The current ratio is 5.3. What is the BEST next step?

A) Raise prices immediately because the current ratio is weak  
B) Invest in new equipment to lower unit costs  
C) Accelerate collections and reduce excess inventory before approving new spending  
D) Switch from MA to FA reporting  

**Answer: C** — High current ratio masks illiquid assets. Cash is trapped in receivables and stock. Fix working capital first (Chapter 6).

---

**Question C**  
NovaCraft evaluates Claude + Slack: Year 1 cost ₹93,600, expected benefit ₹5,76,000. A colleague says "reject it because the Cash Flow Statement shows negative operating cash." What is the BEST response?

A) Negative operating cash means all purchases must be rejected  
B) ROI analysis (MA) supports the purchase if cash can fund the upfront cost; fix Q3 collection issues separately  
C) ROI is irrelevant; only the audited P&L matters  
D) Wait until operating cash is positive for three years  

**Answer: B** — Tool ROI is an MA forward decision. Past operating cash weakness (FA) is a separate problem. Both checks matter; one does not automatically veto the other if cash can fund the purchase and collections are being fixed.

## Formula Reference Sheet

### Financial Statements (Chapter 2)

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Profit / Loss | Profit = Income − Expenses |
| COGS | Opening Inventory + Purchases − Closing Inventory |
| Balance Sheet equation | Assets = Liabilities + Equity |
| Current Ratio | Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities |
| Net Working Capital | Current Assets − Current Liabilities |

### Cost & Contribution (Chapter 3)

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Contribution per unit | Selling Price − Variable Cost per unit |
| Total contribution | Units sold × Contribution per unit |
| Profit | Contribution − Fixed Costs |
| PV Ratio | Contribution ÷ Sales |

### Break-Even (Chapter 4)

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Break-even units | Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit |
| Break-even sales (₹) | Break-even units × Selling Price |
| Profit at volume | (Units × Contribution per unit) − Fixed Costs |

### ROI & Payback (Chapter 5)

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Total cost of purchase | One-time costs + Recurring costs |
| Net benefit | Total expected benefit − Total cost |
| ROI (%) | (Net benefit ÷ Total cost) × 100 |
| Payback period | Total cost ÷ Monthly benefit |

### Early Warning (Chapter 6)

| Concept | Formula / Signal |
|---|---|
| Budget variance | Actual − Budget (favourable if cost is lower) |
| Profit vs cash gap | Compare P&L profit to operating cash flow |
| Funds-flow logic | Profit + non-cash items ± working capital changes ≈ cash movement |
| Credibility trap | Healthy ratios (e.g. current ratio) while cash is low due to receivables/inventory |

### Decision Classification (Chapter 1)

| Signal | Use |
|---|---|
| External audience + past period | Financial Accounting |
| Internal decision + future estimate | Managerial Accounting |

## What to Study Next

You now have a CEO-level finance kit — not a CA qualification. The natural next steps depend on where NovaCraft (or your real company) is on the growth curve.

**If you are pre-revenue or early revenue:**  
Re-read Chapters 3–4 monthly. Break-even and contribution are your survival tools. Keep fixed costs variable as long as possible. Track cash weekly, not quarterly.

**If you are raising your first institutional round:**  
Re-read Chapters 1–2 and 6. Investors read FA; you explain the MA story behind the numbers. Practice walking through your last three statements out loud in under ten minutes. Know why operating cash differed from profit before they ask.

**If you are scaling past 20 people:**  
Deepen variance analysis and departmental budgeting. Add segment reporting — profit per product line, per city, per customer type. Lamba's full book covers funds-flow statements, leverage, and inventory accounting in more depth than this kit.

**Recommended reading (in order):**  
1. *Romancing the Balance Sheet* — Anil Lamba (full book; this kit drew from Chapters 1–3 and 11)  
2. *Financial Intelligence for Entrepreneurs* — Karen Berman & Joe Knight (management-friendly ratios)  
3. *The Lean CFO* — Kurtis Nanette (startup-specific finance operations)

**Practice habit:** Pick one real decision each month. Run the five-step protocol from Chapter 7 on paper. File it. Review after 90 days. That loop — decide, measure, variance-analyze — is how managerial accounting becomes instinct.

You are not a non-finance person anymore. You are a CEO who knows which numbers to trust, when to trust them, and what to do when they disagree.
