# CHAPTER 6: How Do I Spot Problems Before My Accountant Explains Them Away?

---

## 1. THE SITUATION

You are three months from your next fundraise. Revenue is up 18% quarter on quarter. Your accountant sends a clean P&L and says margins are "stable." The board deck looks fine. Then your operations lead mentions that two key suppliers have tightened credit terms. Your collections team is chasing invoices that are 45 days past due. Payroll cleared last month only because you delayed a vendor payment.

You ask your accountant: "Are we okay?"

They reply with precision: "Net profit is positive. Depreciation is non-cash. Receivables timing is normal for your sector. Working capital will normalize."

Every word may be technically correct. None of it tells you whether you will run out of cash in sixty days.

This is the **credibility trap**: the gap between financial statements that are *accurate* and financial reality that is *actionable*. Accountants are trained to record, classify, and comply. Founders are paid to survive, allocate scarce cash, and make decisions under uncertainty. When you rely only on lagging reports and professional reassurance, you discover problems when options are already narrow—during a crunch, a down round, or an emergency bridge.

This chapter gives you a founder's early-warning toolkit: how to read a balance sheet, track working capital, separate profit from cash, run simple variance analysis, and follow the **funds flow** so you see trouble while you can still fix it.

---

## 2. WHY THIS MATTERS

Startups rarely die because they forgot to book revenue. They die because:

- **Cash runs out** while the P&L still shows a profit.
- **Working capital silently absorbs** every rupee of growth.
- **Costs drift** faster than anyone updates the plan.
- **Balance sheet stress** hides behind "one-time" adjustments and footnotes.

Spotting these patterns early changes your leverage. You can renegotiate terms before suppliers cut you off. You can fix pricing before discounting becomes a habit. You can cut burn with surgical precision instead of panic layoffs.

You do not need to become an accountant. You need to ask better questions *before* the monthly close becomes a narrative. The goal is not to catch your accountant in a mistake. The goal is to build **financial reflexes**—a short set of checks you run every month that surface divergence between story and substance.

Three outcomes when you do this well:

1. **Earlier intervention** — You act in weeks, not after a qualified audit opinion.
2. **Better capital decisions** — You know whether you need equity, debt, or operational fixes.
3. **Credibility with investors** — You explain variances yourself instead of being explained to.

## 3. THE THEORY

### 3.1 The Credibility Trap

The credibility trap appears when **technical correctness replaces managerial clarity**. Statements prepared under accounting standards reflect economic events with accrual timing, estimates, and policy choices. That is not the same as **liquidity**, **runway**, or **unit economics under stress**.

Common trap phrases and what they often mask:

| Phrase | What it may obscure |
|--------|---------------------|
| "It's a timing difference" | Persistent working capital drain |
| "Non-cash charge" | Real cash went out elsewhere |
| "Normalized EBITDA" | Costs excluded may return next quarter |
| "Seasonal" | Structural collection or demand weakness |

Your defense is not skepticism for its own sake. It is **triangulation**: compare profit, cash, and balance sheet movement together.

### 3.2 Profit vs Cash

**Profit** (net income) measures performance over a period using accrual accounting. Revenue is recognized when earned; expenses when incurred—whether or not cash moved.

**Cash flow** measures actual rupees in and out. A startup can be profitable and illiquid, or loss-making but cash-rich (e.g., after a fundraise).

The bridge between them lives largely on the **balance sheet**:

- Revenue booked → **Accounts receivable** (cash not yet received)
- Expenses booked → **Accounts payable** or **accrued expenses** (cash not yet paid)
- Inventory purchased → **Inventory** (cash already out, profit not yet earned)

Hence the founder's version of a classic line: **profit is an opinion; cash is a fact**.

### 3.3 Working Capital and the Current Ratio

**Working capital** = Current Assets − Current Liabilities

It is the capital tied up in the short operating cycle: inventory, receivables, prepaid expenses, minus what you owe suppliers and others in the next twelve months.

**Current ratio** = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

Interpretation (manufacturing and trading contexts; SaaS differs):

- **Below 1.0** — Short-term obligations exceed liquid resources; stress unless cash is incoming and reliable.
- **1.0 to 1.5** — Tight but workable if collections are strong.
- **Above 1.5** — More buffer; very high ratios may mean idle cash or poor receivable/collection management.

For growth companies, watch not just the level but the **trend**. Rising revenue with falling current ratio often signals that growth is **self-financing** through receivables and inventory—a classic pre-crisis pattern.

**Quick ratio** (acid-test) = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities — stricter, useful when inventory is slow-moving.

### 3.4 Funds Flow (Sources and Uses)

**Funds-flow analysis** asks: where did money come from, and where did it go?

At a simplified level for founders:

**Sources of funds**

- Operating profit (before non-cash items)
- Increase in current liabilities (e.g., stretching payables)
- New equity or debt
- Sale of fixed assets

**Uses of funds**

- Operating losses
- Increase in current assets (receivables, inventory)
- Capex and asset purchases
- Debt repayment
- Dividends or buybacks

If uses consistently exceed internal sources, external funding or cost reduction is not optional—it is arithmetic.

A monthly **sources-and-uses table** (even one page) beats a thirty-page annual report for decision-making.

### 3.5 Variance Analysis

**Variance** = Actual − Budget (or Actual − Prior Period)

Split variances into:

- **Volume variance** — Did we sell/produce more or less than planned?
- **Price/rate variance** — Did we achieve planned price or cost per unit?
- **Mix variance** — Did the blend of products/channels change?
- **Fixed cost variance** — Did overhead drift?

For startups, the highest-signal report is often the **cost report with variance analysis**: budget vs actual by line item, with **favorable (F)** and **unfavorable (U)** flags—not to assign blame, but to see **where the plan broke**.

A 10% revenue beat means little if COGS ran 22% over plan because of scrap, freight, or vendor price increases nobody escalated.

### 3.6 Reading the Balance Sheet as a Founder

The balance sheet is a **snapshot of claims and resources** at a date—not a flow. Read it in layers:

**Layer 1 — Liquidity (top)**  
Cash, bank balances, receivables, inventory. Ask: How much is truly available within 30 days?

**Layer 2 — Operating cycle**  
Compare receivable days, inventory days, and payable days. Are you financing customers longer than suppliers finance you?

**Layer 3 — Solvency and structure**  
Debt vs equity, related-party balances, contingent liabilities. Growth funded by short-term debt is fragile.

**Layer 4 — Quality of assets**  
Old receivables, obsolete inventory, capitalized software that may need impairment—each can inflate apparent health.

Cross-check: **Balance sheet movement + P&L + cash flow statement** should tell one coherent story. If they do not, that is your signal to dig—not to defer.

## 4. HOW IT WORKS

Use a **monthly founder finance ritual** (60–90 minutes):

### Step 1: Cash truth first

Open the bank statement and cash flow summary. Note:

- Opening cash
- Operating cash in/out
- Closing cash
- Runway at current net burn

Ignore profit for the first ten minutes.

### Step 2: Working capital dashboard

Track four numbers month over month:

| Metric | Formula |
|--------|---------|
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities |
| Receivable days | (Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 30 |
| Inventory days | (Inventory ÷ COGS) × 30 |
| Payable days | (Payables ÷ COGS) × 30 |

Plot trends. One bad month is noise; three consecutive moves in the wrong direction is a pattern.

### Step 3: Profit vs cash reconciliation (simplified)

Take net profit and adjust:

```
Net profit
+ Depreciation and other non-cash charges
− Increase in receivables
− Increase in inventory
+ Increase in payables
≈ Approximate operating cash impact
```

Compare to actual operating cash flow. Large gaps mean something is missing—prepayments, GST timing, capex in wrong bucket, or related-party movements.

### Step 4: Variance analysis on costs

For each major cost line (people, cloud, logistics, marketing, contractors):

- Actual vs budget ₹ and %
- Classify: volume, rate, or one-time
- Owner assigned for next month

Escalate only **unfavorable trends**, not single blips.

### Step 5: Funds-flow one-pager

Summarize:

- Did working capital absorb or release cash?
- Did we raise or repay debt?
- Did capex spike?

End with: **Surplus or deficit this month, and cumulative for the quarter.**

### Step 6: Questions for your accountant (before they explain)

Bring specifics:

- "Receivables rose ₹X but revenue rose only ₹Y—who are the top five debtors and what is overdue?"
- "Current ratio moved from 1.4 to 1.1—was it payables, debt, or receivables?"
- "Which variances over 10% are structural vs one-off?"

You are not auditing them. You are **forcing linkage** between numbers and operations.

## 5. WORKED EXAMPLE

**NovaKitchen Pvt Ltd** sells smart kitchen appliances online and through two retail chains. You are the founder reviewing October results.

### Given data (₹ in lakhs)

| Item | Sep | Oct | Budget (Oct) |
|------|-----|-----|--------------|
| Revenue | 420 | 510 | 480 |
| COGS | 252 | 336 | 288 |
| Gross profit | 168 | 174 | 192 |
| Operating expenses | 130 | 148 | 135 |
| Net profit | 38 | 26 | 57 |
| Cash (bank) | 85 | 62 | — |
| Accounts receivable | 140 | 210 | — |
| Inventory | 95 | 118 | — |
| Accounts payable | 110 | 105 | — |
| Current liabilities (total) | 160 | 175 | — |
| Current assets (total) | 330 | 400 | — |

Depreciation (non-cash) in October: ₹8 lakh.

### Analysis

**1. Profit vs cash**

Net profit fell from ₹38L to ₹26L (−32%). Cash fell from ₹85L to ₹62L (−27%). So the P&L already looks weaker—but cash did not fall *only* because of profit.

Receivables increased by ₹70L (210 − 140). Inventory by ₹23L. Payables **fell** by ₹5L (you paid suppliers faster or ordered less on credit).

Approximate operating cash impact from working capital:

- Receivables up ₹70L → cash **out**
- Inventory up ₹23L → cash **out**
- Payables down ₹5L → cash **out**
- Subtotal working capital drain ≈ **₹98L**

Add back depreciation ₹8L (non-cash). Profit ₹26L plus depreciation ₹8L = ₹34L "paper" generation before working capital—nowhere near enough to offset a ₹98L working capital hit. That explains why cash dropped sharply despite "still profitable."

**2. Current ratio**

September: 330 ÷ 160 = **2.06**  
October: 400 ÷ 175 = **2.29**

Ratio improved—but misleadingly. Receivables and inventory (less liquid, quality risk) drove asset growth, not cash. **Superficially healthy ratio, deteriorating cash.**

**3. Variance analysis (October)**

| Line | Actual | Budget | Variance | Note |
|------|--------|--------|----------|------|
| Revenue | 510 | 480 | +30 F | Volume and one retail promotion |
| COGS | 336 | 288 | +48 U | Higher material cost + air freight for stockout |
| OpEx | 148 | 135 | +13 U | Overtime and contractor surge |
| Net profit | 26 | 57 | −31 U | Revenue beat did not cover cost overruns |

Revenue variance is **favorable**. COGS variance is **unfavorable** and larger. This is the classic **growth trap**: top line beats plan; unit economics worsen.

COGS rate: 336/510 = **65.9%** vs budget 288/480 = **60%**. You sold more but at worse margin—promotional mix and expedited freight.

**4. Funds flow (October, simplified)**

| Sources | ₹L | Uses | ₹L |
|---------|-----|------|-----|
| Net profit | 26 | Δ Receivables | 70 |
| Depreciation (add back) | 8 | Δ Inventory | 23 |
| | | Δ Payables (reduction) | 5 |
| | | Δ Cash (net decrease) | 23* |

*Cash change 85 → 62 = 23; rounding and other WC items may apply in full books.

**Diagnosis before the accountant softens it:** October was not a "good growth month." It was a month of **subsidized revenue** funded by working capital and margin compression. Without action, November collections must fund October's story—or cash worsens.

**5. Founder actions**

- Stop promotion until COGS rate back under 62%.
- Collections sprint on top five receivable accounts (₹210L outstanding).
- Renegotiate supplier terms to restore payables days.
- Weekly 13-week cash forecast; tie marketing spend to collected cash, not booked revenue.

You spotted the problem **from the balance sheet and variances**, not from net profit alone.

### Multiple choice

**Q15.** A manufacturing startup notices declining profits. Which information would most effectively help management identify the cause?

A) Statement of Shareholders' Equity  
B) Cost Report and Variance Analysis  
C) Annual Income Statement only  
D) Auditor's Report  

**Answer: B — Cost Report and Variance Analysis**

**Explanation:** Declining profits can stem from many operational causes—rising material costs, labour overruns, unfavourable product mix, scrap, freight spikes, or fixed overhead creep. A **cost report with variance analysis** compares actual costs to budget (or standard) by line item and classifies deviations as volume, rate, or mix effects. That tells management *where* the plan broke and *what* to fix.

- **A) Statement of Shareholders' Equity** tracks ownership changes (share issues, retained earnings, dividends). It does not explain why costs rose or margins fell.
- **C) Annual Income Statement only** shows that profit declined but not *why*. Without variance detail, you see the symptom, not the diagnosis.
- **D) Auditor's Report** attests to whether statements were prepared fairly—it is a compliance opinion, not an operational diagnostic tool.

When profits fall for three consecutive quarters, request variance analysis before accepting "market conditions" as the full explanation.

**Q16.** The primary purpose of funds-flow analysis for a startup CEO is to:

A) Calculate tax depreciation  
B) Identify where cash was generated and consumed  
C) Determine share fair value  
D) Replace the need for a cash flow statement  

**Answer: B — Identify where cash was generated and consumed**

Funds-flow analysis maps sources (profit, borrowing, payables stretch) against uses (receivables build, inventory, capex, debt repayment). It answers the CEO's real question: where did the money go?

---

**Q17.** A rising current ratio caused mainly by a jump in accounts receivable most likely indicates:

A) Stronger cash position  
B) Improved supplier terms  
C) Revenue booked faster than cash collected  
D) Lower inventory risk  

**Answer: C — Revenue booked faster than cash collected**

Receivables rising faster than cash means customers owe you more. The ratio can look healthier while liquidity worsens.

---

**Q18.** An unfavorable labor rate variance in manufacturing most directly suggests:

A) Fewer units produced than planned  
B) Higher wage or contractor cost than standard  
C) Lower sales volume  
D) Excess cash on hand  

**Answer: B — Higher wage or contractor cost than standard**

A rate variance isolates the price side of labour—overtime, wage hikes, or expensive contractors versus the standard cost built into the plan.

---

**Q19.** Quick ratio excludes inventory because:

A) Inventory is a liability  
B) Inventory may not be quickly convertible to cash  
C) Inventory is always zero in services firms  
D) Auditors require it  

**Answer: B — Inventory may not be quickly convertible to cash**

The quick ratio tests near-term liquidity using assets you can realistically collect or spend within weeks. Inventory may sit unsold for months.

---

**Q20.** The best triangulation to escape the credibility trap is:

A) P&L only, audited annually  
B) Cash book only  
C) P&L, balance sheet changes, and cash flow together  
D) Management presentation slides  

**Answer: C — P&L, balance sheet changes, and cash flow together**

No single statement tells the full story. Profit shows performance, the balance sheet shows what is tied up or owed, and cash flow shows what actually moved. Together they surface gaps that any one report alone can hide.
