# Market Narrative Intelligence — Presentation Draft

## Purpose

This is a detailed story and content draft for a university-grant or incubator presentation. It is deliberately more complete than the eventual deck. The final deck should use little text, one clear visual idea per slide, and only claims that the available evidence supports.

The presentation does **not** claim product-market fit, a validated moat, or a mature venture case. It presents a specific product hypothesis, the market logic behind it, and a disciplined real-world test.

## Evidence labels

- **[Fact]**: Supported by the research notes or a source cited there.
- **[Inference]**: A conclusion drawn from facts and the proposed product logic.
- **[Hypothesis]**: A belief the pilot must test.
- **[Story]**: An attractive claim that the presentation must not use as fact.

## Core presentation position

> **Market Narrative Intelligence is the system of record for a company’s promises to its market. It connects the promise a company makes, the proof it offers, the doubts buyers voice, and the qualified revenue outcome, so each campaign improves the next one.**

### The contrarian belief

> **Marketing and sales are two phases of one commercial promise.**

Buyers do not experience a marketing department, then a sales department, then a CRM. They experience one company making a claim, trying to prove it, answering doubt, and asking for a commercial commitment. Company systems split that experience into functional fragments. The proposed product reconnects them.

### What this presentation asks the audience to believe

1. B2B teams currently lose commercially useful learning when information moves between marketing, sales, and revenue systems.
2. The company promise is a better unit of market learning than the isolated lead, campaign, or sales call.
3. A background system can maintain a traceable promise-to-outcome record with limited human maintenance after integration.
4. That record can improve campaign choices and qualified-revenue outcomes.
5. A small, instrumented pilot can prove or disprove these claims.

## Presentation rules

- Use the same buyer example across Slides 2, 3, 4, and 9. It gives the presentation a human spine.
- Say **“hypothesis”** when describing unvalidated product advantage, demand, price, or moat.
- Name competitors accurately. Do not claim that no existing vendor can do this.
- Do not use a market-size number until it has a source, a defined geography, and a named initial customer.
- Keep one key idea per slide. The speaker carries detail. The slide makes the point memorable.
- Use a calm, editorial visual style. Avoid a dashboard collage, dense UI mockups, and feature grids. The story is about one buyer experience becoming one learning record.

**Market Narrative Intelligence**

*How B2B teams learn which promises create qualified revenue*

## Slide 2 — One buyer experiences one promise

### Slide purpose

**A lead-generation agency makes a promise**

It creates an expectation in the buyer’s mind. The buyer will test the expectation long before they sign a contract.”

## Slide 3 — Buyers test the promise under pressure

### Slide purpose

Show that the sales conversation is an extension of marketing, because buyer doubt tests the original promise.

### On-slide copy

**The sales call tests the same promise**

The buyer asks:

- “What does qualified mean in practice?”
- “Can you show me comparable results?”
- “We hired an agency before and received poor-fit leads. Why will this be different?”
- “Do we need to hire someone internally to make this work?”

### Speaker notes

“These are not random sales objections. Each one tests a part of the initial promise. ‘Qualified’ tests the definition of value. ‘Comparable results’ tests proof. The prior-agency objection tests credibility. The hiring question tests the constraint in the original message. Sales hears these objections, but marketing often never sees their pattern in a form that changes the next campaign.”

### Visual placeholder

`[Visual: A short fictional call-transcript excerpt. Highlight the four phrases that test the promise. Keep it readable from the back of a room.]`

### Evidence status

**[Inference]** Buyer objections can reveal promise weakness or missing proof. The pilot must test whether this linkage repeats across real teams.

### Critique question

Does the audience now see why campaign copy and sales-call evidence belong in one learning system?

## Slide 4 — The buyer’s story breaks across company systems

### Slide purpose

Name the costly operational failure precisely. This slide replaces the vague label “What breaks today?”

### On-slide copy

**The company loses the buyer’s story at the handoff**

Marketing records campaign replies.

Sales records objections and call notes.

The CRM records opportunity status and revenue.

The company rarely connects all three back to the original promise.

### Speaker notes

“The agency can report reply rate. The sales team can remember objections. The CRM can report that an opportunity was lost. The original promise, the proof used, the buyer’s doubt, and the commercial result sit in different records and different conversations. A team may learn that a campaign had low conversion. It often cannot learn whether the weakness came from the promise, the proof, the target segment, the qualification rule, or how the sales team carried the message.”

### Visual placeholder

`[Visual: One buyer journey shown as a continuous line. Place Marketing, Sales, and CRM as disconnected fragments beneath it. The broken links should look intentional and simple.]`

### Evidence status

**[Fact]** Major tools already cover elements of this flow, including CRM, conversation intelligence, campaign analysis, and attribution. See the competitive landscape.

**[Hypothesis]** Existing workflows fail to preserve a usable promise-to-outcome record for the chosen initial customer.

### Critique question

Can a panel member name the specific loss here without hearing the speaker notes?

## Slide 5 — The promise is the unit of market learning

### Slide purpose

State the contrarian insight cleanly. This is the intellectual center of the presentation.

### On-slide copy

**Marketing and sales are two phases of one commercial promise**

Teams currently organise information around functions: campaigns, contacts, calls, and deals.

Buyers respond to a different unit: a promise, the proof behind it, and the doubts it creates.

### Speaker notes

“The common view treats the marketing funnel and the sales funnel as separate systems that need better handoffs. Our starting belief is more radical. The handoff itself is artificial from the buyer’s perspective. The buyer heard one promise. The company needs to learn whether that promise created qualified demand. That makes the promise, not the isolated campaign or call, the right unit of learning.”

### Evidence status

**[Hypothesis]** This is the core product insight. It should be framed as the proposition the project will test.

## Slide 6 — The Promise Record

### Slide purpose

Make the product feel concrete. The Promise Record is the product’s distinctive data object.

### On-slide copy

**Every market promise becomes a record the company can learn from**

| Field | What the system records |
| --- | --- |
| Audience | Which buyer segment received the promise |
| Promise | What outcome and constraint the company claimed |
| Proof | Case study, data point, testimonial, or demonstration |
| Asset | Campaign, page, email, deck, or talk track carrying the claim |
| Buyer response | Reply, meeting request, question, or objection |
| Qualification | Whether the buyer fits the agreed commercial standard |
| Outcome | Opportunity created, lost reason, closed revenue, or retention signal |

### Speaker notes

“A Promise Record starts with an explicit commercial claim and follows it through the buyer journey. The system links the claim to the evidence used to support it, the asset that carried it, the buyer’s response, and the outcome. The record does not merely store a transcript or a campaign report. It gives the company a way to ask: ‘When we say this to this segment, what tends to happen next, and why?’”

### Visual placeholder

`[Visual: A single example Promise Record for the Slide 2 lead-generation example. Use one clean record, not a dashboard.]`

### Evidence status

**[Hypothesis]** The record structure is a product design hypothesis. It requires customer reconstruction before implementation.

### Critique question

Which fields are essential for v1, and which would create unneeded data-collection friction?

## Slide 7 — A background learning system

### Slide purpose

Explain the API-first operating model without turning the presentation into an architecture lecture.

### On-slide copy

**Connect the systems once. Learn from every campaign thereafter.**

The system connects to:

- CRM and opportunity outcomes
- Campaign assets and engagement signals
- Recorded sales calls and written replies

It extracts candidate promises, proof, and objections. A marketer or sales lead approves material campaign decisions. The system continues to update the Promise Record in the background.

### Speaker notes

“The product should not become another dashboard that someone must maintain after every call. The long-term experience is API-first and background-native. After the team connects its existing sources, the system captures the evidence trail automatically. It can infer candidate promises from marketing assets and recurring objections from calls. Humans keep control of high-stakes changes, including what claim goes public and what response becomes approved guidance.”

### Visual placeholder

`[Visual: Four small source labels feeding one central Promise Record, then one output labelled “Next campaign brief.” Keep the visual sparse.]`

### Evidence status

**[Hypothesis]** Automated extraction and low-maintenance use are product requirements. The pilot should begin with three sources only: CRM, recorded calls, and one campaign source.

### Critique question

Does “background-native” sound like a concrete user benefit, or an implementation detail? If it is an implementation detail, move it to speaker notes.

## Slide 8 — The next campaign starts with market evidence

### Slide purpose

Show a complete learning cycle. This slide provides the emotional payoff of the product story.

### On-slide copy

**The next campaign starts with what the market taught the company**

Illustrative learning from the lead-generation example:

| What the system observes | What the team changes |
| --- | --- |
| “Five qualified leads” earns replies but triggers concern about lead quality | Define qualification in the marketing asset and show the filtering process |

### Speaker notes

“The output is not a generic content draft. It is a commercial decision. The system connects recurring evidence to a change in the next campaign. In this example, the agency learns that the quantity claim attracts attention, but trust breaks at the definition of quality and proof. The next asset changes the promise and proof rather than simply adding more follow-up messages.”

### Visual placeholder

`[Visual: Before-and-after campaign headline and proof block. Label the example “Illustrative.”]`

### Evidence status

**[Illustrative example]** The changes make product logic tangible. The pilot must determine whether teams actually receive this kind of learning and use it.

### Critique question

Does the example show a decision the team could not reach just by reading a generic call summary?

## Slide 9 — Current tools own fragments of the journey

### Slide purpose

Show maturity about competition. Do not pretend that existing vendors lack sophisticated capabilities.

### On-slide copy

**Current tools hold valuable evidence. They do not necessarily own the promise across the journey.**

| Existing system | Primary job | Product hypothesis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CRM | Store contacts, pipeline, and commercial outcomes | It rarely makes the marketing promise a first-class record |
| Conversation intelligence such as Gong | Capture conversations, objections, coaching signals, and deal context | It begins after a conversation and centres sales execution |
| Marketing automation and analytics | Launch, measure, attribute, and optimise campaigns | It measures campaign performance without necessarily preserving the commercial claim and later buyer evidence as one record |
| Market Narrative Intelligence | Connect promise, proof, buyer reaction, qualification, and outcome | It creates a recurring market-learning record for the next campaign |

### Speaker notes

“Gong is a serious competitor and a useful source of evidence. Its public positioning includes call capture, objection analysis, coaching, and deal context. CRM and marketing suites also cover important pieces of the flow. Our claim is not that they contain no relevant features. Our claim is narrower: the chosen customer may lack a system that treats the market promise itself as the object that flows across campaign, conversation, qualification, and commercial outcome. If Gong, HubSpot, or a capable internal workflow already solves this for the initial customer, the thesis fails or needs a sharper wedge.”

### Visual placeholder

`[Visual: A buyer journey with the existing tools visibly owning separate segments and the Promise Record spanning the full line. Use text labels rather than competitor logos unless permissions and design justify logos.]`

### Evidence status

**[Fact]** The competitor research confirms that data unification, campaign analytics, conversation analysis, content generation, and attribution are crowded.

**[Hypothesis]** A promise-to-outcome operating record remains an unserved or poorly served workflow for the selected customer.

### Critique question

Would Gong or HubSpot be able to build this as a feature within one release cycle? If yes, what customer workflow, data model, or distribution advantage would still make the product worth using?

## Slide 10 — Qualified revenue creates the value

### Slide purpose

Connect the product to commercial economics. Do not imply strict causality before the pilot.

### On-slide copy

**A stronger commercial promise improves the value of existing demand activity**

```text
Targeted accounts reached
× qualified response or booked-call rate
× opportunity-to-close rate
× annual gross profit per closed customer
= commercial value influenced
```

The system aims to improve the quality of the buyer response and the decision quality of the next campaign. It does not claim that every revenue outcome comes from copy alone.

### Speaker notes

“The commercial value sits in qualified revenue, not in a prettier dashboard. A team already spends money and time to reach a target market. If better promise, proof, and objection handling create more qualified opportunities from the same activity, the value compounds through the sales process. The pilot must collect customer-specific baseline data before placing numbers in this model. We will report influence and measured conversion change, not claim perfect causal attribution.”

### Visual placeholder

`[Visual: A simple editable funnel with four stages and variables. Use no arbitrary benchmark percentage. Later replace variables with one pilot’s actual values.]`

### Evidence status

**[Fact]** Qualified pipeline and closed revenue matter commercially.

**[Hypothesis]** Promise-level learning changes qualified conversion enough for a customer to pay.

### Required numbers before the final presentation

1. Typical monthly targeted-account or campaign volume for the selected customer.
2. Baseline qualified response, booked-call, and opportunity conversion rates.
3. Average gross profit or contribution margin per closed customer.
4. A realistic value-capture model, such as a monthly subscription or pilot fee.

## Slide 11 — The initial customer and the larger market

### Slide purpose

Prove focus while leaving a disciplined expansion path. This slide cannot stay vague.

### On-slide copy

**Initial customer hypothesis**

> B2B demand-generation teams at growth-stage companies that run recurring campaigns, use a CRM, record sales calls, and depend on qualified pipeline.

**Why this group first**

- They produce the evidence sources that the product needs.
- They make recurring campaign decisions, allowing rapid learning.
- They can connect poor message quality to pipeline and revenue consequences.

**Potential expansion, only if the same Promise Record transfers**

Demand generation → product marketing → sales enablement → customer success and renewal messaging.

### Speaker notes

“The long-term category could include many marketing teams. The first test cannot. This target is a working hypothesis, not a final ICP. We choose it because it contains a recurring campaign decision, recorded buyer evidence, a measurable downstream outcome, and a person accountable for pipeline. Expansion becomes credible only if the same Promise Record, integrations, and decision logic work in adjacent workflows. ‘All marketing teams’ is a destination, not an initial market.”

`[Visual: A small initial circle for the first customer and a restrained expansion path. Label each future step “requires proof.”]`

### Evidence status

**[Hypothesis]** The initial customer still requires validation through accessible interviews and real data access.

## Slide 12 — The grant-funded pilot

### Slide purpose

Turn the project into a practical experiment with limited scope, clear outputs, and useful learning even if it fails.

### On-slide copy

**A three-source pilot with two or three B2B teams**

| Phase | Work | Evidence created |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Reconstruct | Review recent campaigns, selected sales calls, CRM outcomes, and objections | Baseline Promise Records and a map of current handoffs |
| 2. Run | Create evidence-backed briefs for one live campaign cycle | Approved campaign decisions, source links, and a record of revisions |
| 3. Compare | Compare the normal workflow, a capable general-AI workflow, and the prototype | Decision-quality comparison, workflow use, and qualified-conversion signal |

### Speaker notes

“The grant does not fund an attempt to build a complete replacement for HubSpot, Gong, or Adobe. It funds a bounded test of the central learning loop. We will begin with CRM, recorded calls, and one campaign source. We will reconstruct historical examples before using a live campaign. Then we will compare the normal process, a careful general-AI baseline, and the prototype. The comparison protects us from mistaking a polished interface for value.”

`[Visual: Three simple numbered phases with one evidence artifact under each. Use a time line only if dates are known.]`

### Evidence status

**[Hypothesis]** The proposed pilot design tests the highest-risk product assumptions at low scope.

## Slide 13 — What the pilot must prove

### Slide purpose

Demonstrate intellectual honesty and prevent the panel from treating the project as vague AI optimism.

### On-slide copy

**The pilot earns the right to continue only if it changes a real marketing decision**

| Claim | Signal that supports it | Result that weakens it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teams have a recurring promise decision | Teams can reconstruct several recent comparable campaigns | Decisions are rare or too idiosyncratic |
| The Promise Record adds value | Teams use it to change a live campaign decision | Teams treat it as an interesting report and continue as before |
| The system beats the available substitute | It improves traceability, decision quality, or qualified conversion beyond the normal process and a general-AI baseline | A capable analyst plus existing tools produces the same result easily |
| The workflow can become a product | Similar teams share the same inputs, record structure, and decision ritual | Every customer needs bespoke research and implementation |

### Speaker notes

“A strong early project needs a way to fail honestly. We will not interpret compliments, demo excitement, or dashboard usage as product validation. We need repeated decisions, actual behaviour change, and a measurable advantage over the customer’s current stack. A negative result gives us a useful conclusion: whether the problem is data access, workflow adoption, generic-AI parity, or service-heavy delivery.”

### Visual placeholder

`[Visual: A clean decision table or a single “Continue / Redesign / Stop” decision path. Avoid a crowded risk matrix.]`

### Evidence status

**[Hypothesis]** These thresholds need numerical values once the customer, time frame, and baseline are known.

# Appendix A — Questions the presentation must answer

## Customer and pain

1. Which exact B2B teams make this decision often enough to create learning?
2. What commercial loss follows when the company uses the wrong promise or proof?
3. Who uses the product, who pays, who owns the data, and who can block adoption?

## Product and trust

4. How does the system infer a promise without creating a manual tagging burden?
5. How does it distinguish a meaningful objection from a one-off comment?
6. How does it handle conflicting evidence or high-risk marketing claims?
7. Which source systems are required for v1, and what data may remain outside the initial scope?

## Competition and differentiation

8. Why can the selected customer not solve this with Gong, HubSpot, Salesforce, a BI analyst, and a general LLM?
9. Could an incumbent add a Promise Record feature quickly?
10. What makes the record valuable enough that the company uses it every campaign?

## Economics and market

11. What increase in qualified pipeline or decision quality would justify the price?
12. How large is the reachable initial segment, using a bottom-up account count?
13. Which shared asset allows expansion beyond the initial customer without rebuilding the product and sales motion?

## Validation

14. What observation would make the team stop, redesign, or narrow the hypothesis?
15. What result would make the team productise the workflow rather than continue delivering bespoke analysis?

# Appendix B — Research and content still required

## Required before presenting this as more than a grant-stage hypothesis

1. **Initial customer decision**: Choose one reachable B2B segment and a named workflow.
2. **Customer reconstruction**: Interview at least 8–10 relevant people about recent campaigns, promises, objections, and outcomes. Do not pitch the product in the first interviews.
3. **Market model**: Build a bottom-up market estimate based on named account criteria, geography, annual price, and reachable share.
4. **Economic baseline**: Collect campaign volume, qualified-conversion rate, sales conversion, and average gross profit from at least one pilot candidate.
5. **Competitor teardown**: Test whether Gong, HubSpot, Salesforce, or a general-LLM workflow already recreates the exact Promise Record and decision ritual.
6. **Data and privacy design**: Specify consent, access control, retention, audit trail, and the restriction against using customer data across clients without permission.
7. **Pilot offer**: Define scope, price or grant subsidy, customer commitment, data access, output cadence, and continuation condition.

- Market-sizing assumptions and account list method.
- Architecture diagram, integration plan, and data-permission model.
- Detailed pilot metrics and risk register.
- Source list from the VC-pitch and competitor research notes.
